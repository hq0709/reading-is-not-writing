# 1. Summary

This paper studies whether linear probe directions provide selective control over the clinical concepts they decode from medical vision-language models. It distinguishes readability, answerability, and “ownership,” where a concept’s direction must increase its own answer more than competing clinical directions while exceeding random and sham references. The evaluation examines 21 checkpoints across two chest-radiograph datasets and a COCO object control, yielding 348 scored write cells. The reported clinical ownership rate is 12%, compared with 56% readability and 62% answerability, while COCO ownership reaches 88%. The authors supplement this comparison with patient-disjoint replication, alternative direction constructions, dose and prompt analyses, probe refits, and numerical checks. Shared-tower comparisons and directions fitted to the model’s own answers provide additional evidence about downstream sensitivity. The empirical question and evaluation scale are valuable, but problems with alternative-direction geometry, clinical label support, numerical validation, and the identification of the proposed mechanism currently prevent a confident endorsement.

# 2. Strengths

1. **The competing-direction comparison exposes failures that random controls miss.**  
   Sections 3.3 and 5.2, Figure 4, and Table 22 provide a concrete example: the Effusion write increases the Effusion answer by 0.190 and clears the random and sham references, yet the Nodule write increases it by 0.262. This is an informative addition to evaluations that report only intervention magnitude.

2. **The campaign provides substantial comparative evidence.**  
   Section 4 and Tables 1 and 4 cover general-purpose and medical checkpoints, multiple model sizes, two clinical datasets, and a natural-image control. Reporting the readable-and-answerable subset separately in Section 5.1 is particularly useful: ownership remains uncommon even among these cells.

3. **The seed result has an independent patient replication.**  
   Section 5.2 and Table 22 report a new-patient ownership estimate of −0.071 with a tight interval, consistent with the earlier −0.057 estimate. Locking the dose before evaluating the replication strengthens this result.

4. **The shared-tower comparisons establish meaningful downstream dependence.**  
   Section 5.4, Figure 5, and Table 14 show substantial response differences when readers consume identical or nearly identical visual features and directions. The bit-identical comparisons are especially persuasive evidence that visual probe quality alone cannot predict intervention behavior.

5. **The paper reports several consequential robustness results.**  
   Tables 12–15 and 20–21 go beyond superficial controls. In particular, reporting that only 3 of 10 owned NIH cells retain ownership under both probe refits, versus 87 of 100 COCO cells, makes the instability of clinical ownership visible. The simultaneous clinical contrasts in Section 3.3 and Appendix E are also preferable to selecting and testing a winning competitor afterward.

# 3. Weaknesses

## W1. Clinical label support is insufficient for some reported capability judgments

**Location:** Sections 3.4 and 4; Appendix A.1; Table 5.

The answerability rule requires only that the clean-answer AUROC lower bound exceed 0.5. Table 5 reports **84 positive and only 2 negative test examples for CheXpert Atelectasis**, with 514 unknown labels; training contains only 86 known negatives. The ten-examples-per-class requirement applies to readability but apparently not answerability. With two negative test examples, approximately 13% of ordinary bootstrap resamples contain no negatives, and retaining or dropping those draws materially affects the procedure.

The statement that known-label rows preserve ownership signs in 108/114 cells does not address whether noisy or selectively observed **training labels** produce unreliable clinical directions.

**Evidence needed:** Report class support and valid bootstrap-draw counts for every capability estimate, including the handling of undefined AUROCs. Obtain an independently adjudicated clinical subset with adequate positive and negative support, and repeat both direction fitting and evaluation. Apply explicit support requirements to answerability and regenerate the headline counts. Distinguish blank from uncertain labels in the sensitivity analysis.

## W2. The alternative-direction lifting rule confuses classifier normals with feature displacements

**Location:** Section 5.6; Appendix A.7, lines 938–943; Table 15.

I object to:

> “The same lift carries classifier normals, class-mean displacements, and patterns alike.”

Under the stated preprocessing, let \(D_s=\operatorname{diag}(s)\) and
\[
z=D_s^{-1}(R^\top h-\mu).
\]
A classifier coefficient \(\beta\) correctly maps to the hidden-space gradient \(R D_s^{-1}\beta\). However, a displacement \(\delta z\), such as a difference of means or a Haufe pattern in standardized feature coordinates, requires a different transformation. Its minimum-norm hidden-space realization is
\[
\delta h=R(R^\top R)^{-1}D_s\,\delta z,
\]
assuming full column rank. Applying \(R D_s^{-1}\) to both types of object changes the intended displacement.

Moreover, calling a Gaussian matrix “near-orthonormal” does not establish that \(R^\top R\approx I\) accurately enough for this argument.

**Evidence needed:** Provide the exact preprocessing and lifting implementation. Recompute difference-of-means directions directly in hidden space, and Haufe patterns using the corresponding hidden-space covariance, or implement the appropriate displacement lift. Report the construction and singular values of \(R\), verify the resulting projected displacements numerically, and regenerate Table 15. The current table does not establish robustness to the named alternatives.

## W3. The COCO comparison does not identify clinical semantics as the source of the deficit

**Location:** Abstract; Section 5.3; Figures 2 and 7; Tables 1, 5, and 11–12.

I object to:

> “The deficit is specific to clinical concepts.”

The experiments establish a large difference between the selected chest findings and COCO objects. They do not separate clinical semantics from annotation quality, task difficulty, image modality, spatial scale, representation geometry, or model familiarity. The saturation and co-occurrence analyses address useful individual explanations, but they do not jointly match these factors. In particular, comparable relative activation norms need not correspond to comparable displacement along the data distribution.

**Evidence needed:** Add a crossed control panel: nonpathological attributes or devices in chest radiographs, and subtle or fine-grained concepts in natural images. Compare clinical and natural-image cells matched on raw probe AUROC, clean-answer AUROC, class support, co-occurrence, and baseline response distribution. Repeat the comparison with adjudicated clinical labels and a dose sensitivity analysis calibrated using training/calibration data. These experiments would directly support the proposed clinical specificity.

## W4. The paper does not adequately separate failed steering from demonstrated semantic competition

**Location:** Sections 3.3–3.4 and 5.1; Tables 7–8, 10, and 13.

I object to:

> “only \(O_q>0\) shows that the label names the effective direction.”

Ownership establishes relative superiority within a specified intervention family. Establishing that it identifies a semantic handle additionally requires construct validation: a direction can encode a concept while another direction has greater downstream gain, and a question-specific response can arise through a shortcut.

The empirical decomposition also needs correction. Only **44/234 clinical cells** clear the steering reference. Section 5.1 then states:

> “In 15 of those 44 cells, a competing clinical direction moves the answer at least as much.”

Subtracting 29 owned cells from 44 reference-passing cells does not establish this: failure of a simultaneous lower bound can mean unresolved competition. Tables 7–8 contain dagger-marked entries printed as \(+0.00\), reinforcing the need to distinguish these cases.

**Evidence needed:** Report a complete contingency table separating reference failure, significant clinical advantage, significant stronger competitor, and unresolved comparisons. Repeat it within the readable-and-answerable subset and with a prespecified meaningful competitor-effect floor. Validate ownership using interventions with independently known concept changes, and test whether winning directions preserve concept-specific effects across held-out questions and nuisance controls.

## W5. Fixed batch composition does not establish numerical invariance of intervention effects

**Location:** Section 3.5; Appendix A.3 and A.7; Tables 3, 14, and 19.

I object to:

> “We record these as declared deviations and neutralise them through fixed batch composition.”

Keeping batch composition fixed removes one source of variation; it does not establish that numerical error cancels between clean and intervened conditions. Table 3 reports deviations as large as 1.84 logits. These could interact with intervention direction and reader, particularly in saturated or marginal cells.

Table 19 evaluates only **Qwen2.5-VL-7B on COCO**, using 200 rows. It reports agreement of two point decisions, rather than agreement of the full simultaneous ownership grade. This does not validate the clinical cells or the Gemma comparisons central to the reader argument.

**Evidence needed:** Rescore clinical blocks covering the largest batch discrepancies, the shared-tower comparisons, and marginal owned cells using both batch size one and full-fp32 forward computation. Recompute the complete reference and simultaneous-bound decisions on the same patients. Report changes in \(W\), \(O\), reference thresholds, and grades. Also specify exactly how “fixed batch composition” is implemented.

## W6. Answer-direction rescue does not yet establish a transferable clinical handle

**Location:** Section 5.6; Tables 16 and 20–21.

I object to:

> “This answer direction \(a_q\) is a handle where the label direction is not.”

Fitting clean answer margins and validating the resulting interventions on held-out patients is a useful experiment. However, it identifies sensitivity associated with the fitted answer interface; the semantic interpretation requires additional evidence.

Table 21 contains **11 rescues among 27 previously unowned clinical cells and one loss among nine previously owned cells**. The result is therefore heterogeneous. Furthermore, Table 20 reports covariance-weighted alignment of **0.87 for Lingshu on CheXpert**, with a score correlation of 0.88. This is an important case for explaining the relationship between raw angular separation and functional agreement.

**Evidence needed:** Fit answer directions on one template and evaluate them on held-out wordings, reversed answer mappings, and an additional semantic endpoint. Report answer-direction column selectivity and nuisance effects. Compare label and answer directions against a common family containing both constructions for each competing concept, with construction-specific shams. Match training support, quantify rescue/loss uncertainty, and report alignment–ownership associations within datasets and readers rather than relying on the pooled correlation.

## W7. Shared towers establish reader dependence, but do not isolate the reader as the determining mechanism

**Location:** Abstract; Sections 5.4 and 7; Figure 5; Table 14.

I object to:

> “What a written direction does is decided by the reader of the representation—the connector and language model—not by the representation.”

Holding the representation fixed and changing the downstream model establishes downstream dependence. It does not determine the relative roles of representation, connector, language model, and their interactions. Section 3.1 appropriately describes ownership as a property of the pair; the subsequent causal attribution requires an additional intervention axis.

The assertion that differences exceed “every noise source” also needs reconciliation with Table 14, where a median reader difference is only 0.6 times the refit standard deviation.

**Evidence needed:** Add a controlled tower-by-reader experiment with compatible interfaces or matched trained variants, varying each component independently. Replay exactly the same stored feature tensors for reader comparisons, including the currently approximate bf16 matches. Measure connector and language-model effects separately where feasible, and test whether downstream sensitivity or gradient alignment predicts the observed ownership changes.

## W8. The uncertainty statements cover less than the complete ownership grade

**Location:** Sections 3.3–3.4; Figure 2a; Tables 12–13; Appendix E.

The max-\(T\) procedure covers the 30 clinical contrasts conditional on fitted directions and the reference family. It does not, as specified, jointly quantify uncertainty in the random p95 threshold, absolute sham comparison, or training/refit procedure. Appendix E supplies a lower-bound construction but does not explicitly define the simultaneous upper bounds used for “stronger competitor.”

Table 13 also reuses the CORE sham after changing the fitted probe direction. That is no longer necessarily the coordinate-permutation sham of the direction being evaluated. Finally, bootstrapping cells in Figure 2a ignores dependence from shared patients, concepts, and model families.

**Evidence needed:** Specify the upper-tail procedure and the exact scope of simultaneous coverage. Jointly resample the reference contrasts when assigning inferential grades, regenerate direction-specific shams after refitting, and assess sensitivity to projection and reference-family seeds. Report aggregate uncertainty that preserves shared-patient dependence and accounts for related checkpoints. Retain the distinction between a positive ownership point estimate and retention of the full grade in saturation and refit summaries.

## W9. Coverage and artifact reporting need a reproducible reconciliation

**Location:** Section 4; Reproducibility Statement; Appendix A.1; Tables 1–2, 6, 17–18; Figure 7.

Section 4 states:

> “We grade Llama 3.2 Vision 11B on its eligible templates.”

However, Tables 1 and 6 show incomplete or ineligible primary write matrices and no resulting owned-cell contribution. It is unclear which eligible-template grades actually exist. Table 2 is said to identify consumed blocks, but it lists tower descriptions rather than exact hook/module paths. Table 6 also omits some modules whose results appear elsewhere, including Gemma 3 12B NIH token weighting in Table 18 and Lingshu 32B NIH extended competitors in Table 17.

No anonymous artifact URL, protocol identifier, or registration timestamp appears in the supplied manuscript. The assertion that all campaign cohorts are disjoint from every seed-study cohort also warrants a patient-level overlap audit, including training cohorts.

**Concrete resolution:** Publish one authoritative manifest linking every reported number to its checkpoint, template, cohort, module, protocol version, and source artifact. Supply the anonymous artifact location, frozen protocol and amendments, exact module paths, and patient-overlap counts. Reconcile Llama coverage and all denominators. Correct Figure 7’s CheXpert heatmap label “Nodule” to “Edema,” and clearly distinguish the positive-flow aggregate in Figure 7 from the signed column-selectivity statistic in Table 20.

# 4. Questions for the Authors

1. How are undefined bootstrap AUROCs handled for CheXpert Atelectasis, and which answerability grades depend on its two negative examples?
2. Does the implementation lift mean and pattern **displacements** differently from classifier **coefficients**? Is \(R\) explicitly orthogonalized?
3. Among the 15 reference-passing but unowned clinical cells, how many have a significantly stronger competitor, and how many are unresolved?
4. Do the clinical and shared-tower conclusions survive full-fp32 and batch-size-one rescoring with the complete ownership rule?
5. Do answer directions transfer to templates and semantic endpoints excluded from their fitting procedure?
6. What experiment distinguishes clinical semantics from annotation quality, modality, and task difficulty?
7. Where are the frozen protocol, amendments, complete artifact manifest, and patient-level overlap audit available?
