**1. Summary**

This paper asks whether a direction that linearly decodes a visual concept also provides a selective intervention on the model’s answer about that concept. It introduces readability, answerability, and ownership, with ownership requiring a positive write effect above random and sham references and simultaneous superiority over competing concept directions. Across 21 checkpoints, the authors report ownership in 29/246 chest-finding cells versus 101/120 natural-object cells. The decomposition is informative: only 44 chest cells clear the steering references, and eight of these have a significantly stronger clinical competitor. The paper also replicates its motivating example on new patients and evaluates alternative direction constructions, numerical precision, refits, templates, and intervention locations. Shared-tower comparisons, tower–reader swaps, and directions fitted to clean answer margins investigate why readable and writable directions differ. This is a substantial empirical contribution, but inconsistent matching statistics, unresolved domain confounding, and weaknesses in validating ownership as a semantic and predictive criterion prevent me from accepting the principal explanatory claims as established.

**2. Strengths**

- **A useful comparison beyond random perturbations (§§3.2–3.4; Table 2).** Comparing identically constructed concept directions on the same question, patients, and dose is a meaningful addition to steering evaluations. Table 2 appropriately separates failure to exceed generic references from failure against semantic competitors; these are different empirical phenomena.

- **Broad, systematically paired evaluation (§4; Tables 1, 3, and 7–9).** The checkpoint grid includes general-purpose and medical models, multiple architectures, two radiograph datasets, and a natural-image comparison. Reporting full direction–question matrices makes the results more informative than isolated steering success rates.

- **Independent replication of the motivating counterexample (§5.2; Table 25).** The Effusion direction produces a substantial positive effect, yet the Nodule direction produces a larger effect on the same answer. Replication on new patients, with ownership −0.071 and a confidence interval excluding zero, provides convincing evidence for this particular failure of directional attribution.

- **Extensive investigation of plausible methodological explanations (§5.8; Tables 12–15 and 18).** The paper does considerably more than test logistic normals once. Alternative estimators, displacement lifts, extended competitor sets, token weighting, refits, saturation analyses, and precision checks materially strengthen the descriptive chest–COCO contrast.

- **Strong experiments on reader dependence (§5.5; Tables 14 and 22).** Comparing readers supplied with identical stored activations is especially valuable. These results support the conclusion that differences in intervention response can arise downstream of a shared visual representation.

- **The answer-direction comparison is informative (§5.6; Tables 16 and 19).** Separately measuring prediction of clinical labels and prediction of the model’s clean answers helps distinguish these targets. Reporting column selectivity alongside row ownership also exposes an important limitation of the apparent rescue.

**3. Weaknesses**

1. **The difficulty-matching results are internally inconsistent, and the strongest adjusted comparisons do not establish the claimed conclusion (§5.4; Table 24).**

   The paper states, “Task difficulty does not explain the contrast.” However, Table 24 reports chest and natural ownership rates of 0.118 and 0.840 with a difference of −0.635; their difference is −0.722. The other rows are more problematic: 0.151 versus 0.840 is paired with −0.086, and 0.131 versus 0.729 with −0.104. These could reflect different weighting schemes, but the caption describes the rates and differences as belonging to the same matched comparison. Moreover, the intervals for answer-AUROC matching and joint matching include zero.

   Matching selectivity also does not necessarily match probe quality: selectivity subtracts performance on different nuisance-control tasks in the two domains (§F.4). Finally, the six fine-grained-object blocks own 30/36 fine categories versus **34/36 easy categories in those same blocks**, whereas the headline comparison uses 101/120 easy categories across a different checkpoint set.

   **Resolution:** Define the matched estimand and pair weights mathematically; report rates, differences, effective sample sizes, and intervals using identical weights. Recompute the fine/easy comparison within the same six blocks. Add matching or stratification on raw probe AUROC, report the corresponding control AUROCs and overlap diagnostics, and provide sufficient matched evidence to resolve the currently inconclusive answer-accuracy-adjusted comparisons.

2. **The claimed specificity to clinical concepts conflicts with the within-radiograph control (§§5.4 and 5.8; Table 17).**

   The sentence “The deficit is specific to clinical concepts rather than to task difficulty” is not supported by Table 17. Non-clinical attributes of the same radiographs are owned in 12/114 cells, essentially the same rate as findings in the joint family, 23/228. This persists when restricting to answer-capable cells and when matching answerability. These results leave an image-domain or radiograph-reader explanation open.

   **Resolution:** Supply evidence separating concept category from image domain. A useful additional comparison would include independently annotated, visually localized non-clinical objects or devices within the same radiographs, with reliable clean answers and matched probe quality, alongside natural-image controls. Analyze these together with the existing attribute results and test whether clinical status explains ownership after accounting for domain and measured difficulty. The current attribute experiment must enter the explanatory argument directly.

3. **Row dominance has not yet been adequately validated as a semantic “handle” (§3.3; §5.6; Tables 19 and 23).**

   I object to “only \(O_q>0\) shows that the label names the effective direction” and “The same representation contains the reader’s handle.” A direction can win its own question’s row while substantially moving unrelated answers. Indeed, answer directions improve chest ownership from 9/36 to 19/36, but their median column selectivity is only 0.24 versus 0.19 for label directions.

   The semantic endpoint experiments are valuable, but their results are mixed: the reported both-order forced-choice success is 3/18, and report-continuation ownership is 0/18. Opposite responses to negation provide some supporting evidence, but do not by themselves establish selective control of the clinical concept.

   **Resolution:** Validate ownership against independent semantic outcomes that require both target transfer and preservation of unrelated findings. Establish clean capability for each endpoint, report direction-family-specific order consistency, and evaluate complete finding phrases or semantic report outputs rather than an insufficiently specified “finding word.” Explain the report-continuation failures through endpoint checks or additional successful transfer evidence. Show that owned directions outperform magnitude-matched unowned directions on these independent outcomes.

4. **The incremental predictive-validity analysis does not support its inferential conclusion (§5.6; Appendix A.8; Table 23).**

   The paper states, “Ownership predicts endpoint effects beyond write magnitude, probe selectivity, and clean-answer AUROC,” based on positive increments in \(R^2\). These appear to be training-set \(R^2\) values from nested regressions. Adding predictors cannot decrease training-set \(R^2\), so a positive patient-bootstrap interval is not a convincing test of incremental prediction.

   The appendix correctly identifies only six effective concept-level observations per block. Adding ownership and its indicator to three existing concept-level predictors leaves almost no independent concept-level information for validation; when all predictors are independent, the concept-level component can be saturated. Resampling patients does not supply new concepts.

   **Resolution:** Evaluate incremental performance on held-out concepts or blocks, with all fitting and model selection confined to training folds. Use a null comparison that permutes ownership at the appropriate concept/block level, and account for dependence from estimating predictors and endpoint effects on the same patients. Report held-out performance changes and their uncertainty.

5. **Several robustness results use different rules while being described as the same owned grade (§5.8; Appendices A.7–A.8 and E; Tables 12, 13, 16, and 23).**

   The statement “We grade each endpoint with the campaign’s rule” is difficult to reconcile with applying the random reference only “where the endpoint scores it.” The same qualification appears for held-out answer-direction templates. A sham-only reference is weaker than the primary random-plus-sham reference.

   Table 12 also defines logit-scale ownership using a positive percentile interval for the maximum contrast, whereas the primary grade uses simultaneous bounds on fixed contrasts; Appendix E says the same rules apply. Finally, refits retain seed-0 shams (§F.3), although a coordinate-permutation sham is defined as a transformation of the direction being tested.

   **Resolution:** Provide a condition-level table identifying which references and inferential rules were actually used. Complete missing random-reference evaluations for the semantic endpoints and template-transfer claims, and recompute logit-scale grades with the primary simultaneous procedure. Construct matched shams for refitted directions or quantify whether doing so changes the results. Clearly distinguish empirical reference thresholds from confidence bounds.

6. **The label-source experiment does not separate annotation quality from training-set size (§5.8; Appendix A.7; Tables 19–20).**

   The paper reports that expert-label refits own fewer cells than report-label directions. However, those expert directions are fitted using 200 films rather than approximately 20,000 training rows, and their held-out AUROC is lower, 0.69 versus 0.79. Thus, failure to restore ownership could reflect estimation quality rather than an absence of label-noise effects.

   The 112/114 ownership agreement in Table 20 demonstrates transfer to a new image cohort, but ownership itself does not use the evaluation labels. It therefore cannot independently validate the clinical meaning of directions trained on report labels.

   **Resolution:** Compare expert-label and report-label fits using the same images, sample sizes, regularization procedure, and held-out expert evaluation. Include training-size sensitivity and uncertainty on direction stability and ownership. To resolve the label-noise explanation, demonstrate the result with expert-trained probes that have adequate held-out clinical discrimination.

7. **The NIH sampling description is contradictory, and answer-direction cross-validation does not respect the stated independence unit (§4; §5.6; Table 28).**

   Section 4 says, “Both chest datasets use one frontal study per patient.” Table 28 instead reports 18,212 NIH training rows from 5,783 patients, and §5.6 explicitly uses 3,000 rows from 1,871 patients with row-wise cross-validation. Patients can therefore occur in both training and validation folds during ridge-penalty selection.

   This does not establish leakage into the patient-disjoint test cohort, but it compromises the interpretation of the reported cross-validation performance and may affect penalty selection.

   **Resolution:** Correct the cohort description to identify which roles contain repeated patients. Rerun ridge selection using patient-grouped folds on NIH, report the resulting penalties and ownership results, and assess sensitivity to patient-balanced training weights. Provide an explicit cohort-overlap count table for the campaign and seed-study roles.

8. **The tower–reader crossing does not yet connect its continuous effects to the claimed ownership mechanism (§5.5; Table 21).**

   The paper states, “A crossed tower-and-reader design establishes that the representation and reader jointly determine ownership.” The reader evidence from shared towers is strong. In the crossing, however, every chest arm has zero owned cells, and the reported factor effects concern changes in \(W_{q,q}\), not changes in ownership margins or competitor ordering. Furthermore, replacing a tower also replaces its fitted write direction, so the intervention changes more than the representation-producing component.

   One hybrid also loses all natural-object ownership, making interface compatibility a relevant unresolved explanation.

   **Resolution:** Report paired factor effects on \(O_q\), the underlying competitor contrasts, and the reference margins, with uncertainty. Include clean answerability and representation/connector diagnostics for all hybrid arms. Where coordinates permit, cross the written direction separately from the tower to distinguish tower effects from direction-estimation effects. These measurements would make the mechanism claim assessable beyond changes in absolute write magnitude.

9. **The protocol and coverage descriptions need reconciliation (§3; §4; Appendices A.4 and F.6; Tables 6 and 29).**

   “We choose nothing after observing the outcome” requires a clear record of which choices were fixed when. Amendments are not inherently problematic, but Table 29 does not document several analyses carrying major claims, including the tower crossing, tensor replay, semantic endpoints, fine-grained matching, and expert-label refitting. The pre-grid category-selection “rule” is also asserted without being specified.

   Separately, §4 and Appendix A.4 describe CheXpert as having only write and calibration modules, while Table 6 and subsequent results report additional modules. These inconsistencies make it difficult to distinguish planned coverage, completed coverage, and exploratory extensions.

   **Resolution:** Provide a complete analysis-registration ledger with dates, selection rules, checkpoint coverage, and whether relevant outcomes were already visible. Update the main text and captions from one final coverage table. Explicitly account for excluded cells in robustness denominators, including unsaturated-row analyses.

**4. Questions for the authors**

1. What exact estimand produces the Table 24 “difference” column, and why does it differ from subtracting the displayed ownership rates? Which estimates support the difficulty conclusion after matching clean-answer AUROC?
2. What observation distinguishes a clinical-concept-specific deficit from a radiograph-domain deficit, given the nearly identical attribute and finding ownership rates in Table 17?
3. Which template and semantic-endpoint results were evaluated without random-direction references, and how many owned grades survive the complete primary rule?
4. Does ownership improve prediction on held-out concepts or checkpoints? Are any Table 23 \(R^2\) values evaluated out of sample?
5. How do NIH answer-direction results change under patient-grouped cross-validation, and expert-label results under sample-matched training?
6. What are the clean answerability and ownership-margin contrasts for all four tower–reader combinations, particularly the hybrid with zero COCO ownership?
7. All direction constructions share one 512-dimensional projection. Does the chest–COCO gap persist across independent projection seeds and with higher-dimensional or native-space probes?
