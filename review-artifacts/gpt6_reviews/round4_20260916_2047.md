## 1. Summary

This paper investigates whether linear probe directions that decode clinical findings also provide selective control over a medical vision-language model’s answers. It defines readability, answerability, and ownership, with ownership requiring a concept’s direction to outperform random and sham references and all competing clinical directions at a matched intervention dose. The evaluation covers 21 checkpoints from nine families, comprising 61 dataset–checkpoint blocks and 366 concept cells. The reported ownership rate is 12% on chest findings versus 84% on COCO objects, despite substantially higher clinical readability and answerability. A patient-disjoint replication supports the motivating result that the Nodule direction changes the Effusion answer more than the Effusion direction itself. Shared-tower comparisons and directions fitted to the model’s clean answers provide further evidence that downstream readers influence intervention outcomes. The benchmark and empirical observations are valuable, but the claims about clinical specificity and semantic handles require additional discriminating evidence, and several inconsistencies in the reported analyses need reconciliation.

## 2. Strengths

- **The matched clinical competitors are a useful evaluation contribution (§§3.1–3.4; Table 10).** Comparing directions on the same question, patients, intervention locus, and dose addresses an ambiguity that random perturbations alone cannot resolve. Separating steering-reference success from clinical advantage is particularly useful.

- **The motivating counterexample is quantitatively convincing (§5.2; Table 27).** The Effusion write produces a substantial effect, exceeds the random and sham references, and nevertheless loses to Nodule. The reported ownership interval, −0.071 [−0.080, −0.062], and replication on new patients make this more persuasive than a selected qualitative example.

- **The evaluation has substantial breadth and exposes heterogeneity (§4; Tables 1, 4, and 7–10).** Including general and medical models, size ladders, two chest datasets, and a natural-image control helps establish that the observed pattern is not confined to one checkpoint. Reporting individual concepts and separating unresolved cases from stronger competitors are good practices.

- **The robustness work addresses many plausible objections (§5.6; Tables 13–17 and 23).** Logit-scale checks, training-patient refits, displacement-lifted alternatives, and full-forward precision checks materially strengthen the empirical result. Reporting poor clinical ownership retention under refitting, rather than hiding it behind an average perturbation statistic, is especially informative.

- **The reader analyses provide constructive evidence (§5.4; Tables 15, 18, and 19).** Large ownership differences with identical visual features establish that downstream computation matters. Answer-fitted directions succeeding on held-out patients and transferring across answer mappings are interesting positive results beyond simply documenting failure.

## 3. Weaknesses

### W1. Several numerical and protocol statements cannot be reconciled with the accompanying tables

**Locations:** §5.6; §§A.1, A.4; Tables 6, 11, and 21; Figure 2c.

The sentence

> “Extending the clinical family to every additional dataset label with enough support retains 5 of the 6 owned cells in the 8 scored blocks (Table 21).”

does not match Table 21. That table lists **nine blocks**, with **11 originally owned cells and eight retained cells**.

Coverage also changes depending on the source. Section 4 and Appendix A.4 describe CheXpert as receiving CORE and calibration, whereas Table 6 reports substantial additional coverage and explicitly says that five modules were added after the first wave. Table 11 still omits those CheXpert controls. Figure 2c reports 20 NIH and 19 COCO dose blocks, whereas Table 6 lists dose coverage for 21 and 20, respectively.

These discrepancies matter because the paper presents the evaluation as registered and uniformly reported.

**Resolution:** Regenerate narrative counts, tables, and figures from one frozen coverage manifest. State every analysis’s included blocks and exclusions. Provide protocol identifiers, dates, and amendments distinguishing the original registered campaign from subsequently added modules. Explain the Figure 2c exclusions or update the figure.

### W2. Ownership has not been sufficiently validated as a test of semantic control

**Locations:** Introduction; §§3.3–3.4, 5.1, and 5.6; Tables 10, 18, 19, and 26.

The Introduction states:

> “A concept with a readable but unowned probe direction is one the model can be read for but not written with—reading is not writing.”

The seed result itself demonstrates that an unowned direction can substantially and reproducibly write the answer. Ownership establishes relative superiority within a candidate family; additional evidence is needed to establish when that ranking diagnoses semantic control.

Table 10 also distinguishes two substantially different phenomena. Among 44 chest cells passing the steering reference, 29 are owned, eight have a stronger competitor, and seven are unresolved. Thus, **155 of the 163 stronger-competitor verdicts occur in cells that fail the steering reference**. This does not invalidate the counterexamples, but the aggregate 66% statistic combines meaningful competing interventions with potentially very small or negative own effects.

The answer-direction result is promising but does not independently validate clinical semantics: the fitting target is the model’s own answer. Table 26 implies **11 rescues among 27 label-unowned chest cells**, eight retained successes, and one loss. Table 19’s 76/95 figure measures retention conditional on primary-template ownership; the unconditional held-out-template count is 87/180 chest pairs.

**Resolution:** Validate ownership against independently defined semantic interventions or a controlled system with known concept mechanisms. On representative medical checkpoints, test answer directions and probe directions on held-out semantic tasks beyond the six templates—for example, negated finding questions, distinguishing related findings, and structured finding descriptions. Report answer-direction write matrices and column selectivity. Quantify stronger-competitor cases jointly by effect size, reference success, and readability/answerability, including whether the winning competitor itself clears the relevant reference.

### W3. The evidence does not yet distinguish clinical-concept specificity from radiograph-domain difficulty

**Locations:** Abstract; §5.3; Table 20.

The central sentence

> “The deficit is specific to clinical concepts.”

requires a comparison that separates concept type from image domain. COCO changes the image distribution, annotation process, semantic categories, and model competence simultaneously.

The paper already contains an especially relevant control: Table 20 reports low ownership for **non-clinical attributes of the same radiographs**, including only 2/30 ownership for projection despite approximately 0.999 probe AUROC. This suggests that the difficulty may extend beyond clinical findings. However, those attributes use nine competing directions and lack the random reference, so their rates cannot be directly compared with the six-direction COCO protocol. The table’s “answer AUROC” also measures probe scores against the model’s clean answers, rather than the model’s answerability against attribute truth.

**Resolution:** Run a matched within-radiograph comparison between findings and non-clinical attributes, with identical competitor counts, random/sham references, support requirements, and prompt checks. Report actual attribute answerability. Add a competence-matched comparison with natural-image concepts or attributes, matching label support and clean-answer performance where feasible. Demonstrate that the clinical/non-clinical difference persists after these controls.

### W4. The radiologist-cohort analysis does not test whether training-label noise causes poor directions

**Locations:** §5.6; §A.1; Tables 5, 24, and 25.

The statement

> “Radiologist labels on 200 CheXpert films reproduce ownership in 112 of 114 cells (Table 25).”

is potentially misleading as evidence about label robustness. Once the directions are fixed, ownership depends on images and model responses, not on their evaluation labels. Table 25 therefore provides useful replication on a different patient cohort, but it does not establish that directions fitted using report-derived labels recover the clinically relevant direction.

The known-label restriction in Table 24 also leaves training-label noise unchanged. Separately, Table 5 shows only **two known negative test examples for CheXpert Atelectasis**, making its readability and answerability ineligible under §3.4. Including such cells in headline denominators without separately displaying eligibility makes the clinical–COCO grade comparison harder to interpret.

**Resolution:** For representative concepts and checkpoints, fit directions using an independent expert-labelled training subset and compare them with report-label directions fitted on the same images and sample sizes. Evaluate both on independent expert-labelled patients. Report eligibility separately from failed readability/answerability, and provide eligible-only and support-matched aggregate comparisons. Explicitly identify which conclusions Table 25 tests through new images and which require corrected training labels.

### W5. Shared-tower comparisons establish a reader effect, but do not isolate the cause of the clinical deficit

**Locations:** Abstract; §§3.1 and 5.4; Figure 5; Table 15.

The Abstract states:

> “What a written direction does is decided by the reader of the representation—the connector and language model—not by the representation.”

The shared-tower experiment convincingly shows that changing the reader can change ownership while holding the representation approximately or exactly fixed. It does not compare reader changes against representation changes, nor determine which downstream component produces the clinical–COCO difference. Section 3.1’s formulation as a property of the **representation–reader pair** is more closely connected to the actual experimental design.

**Resolution:** Add a controlled crossed experiment using architecturally compatible base/medical checkpoints: vary the tower while holding the reader fixed, and vary the reader while holding the tower fixed. Replay identical stored tensors and write vectors for the reader comparison. Where possible, separate connector changes from language-model changes. Report whether these interventions predictably create or remove the clinical ownership deficit, beyond merely producing different ownership values.

### W6. Several robustness analyses use different decision rules while being described as preserving the same grade

**Locations:** §§3.4 and 5.6; Tables 13, 14, 19, and 24; Appendix E.

For example:

> “Restricting each cell to unsaturated rows preserves all 29 owned chest cells.”

Table 13 establishes positive ownership **point estimates** for those cells, not preservation of the complete reference-plus-simultaneous-bound grade. Its logit-scale caption refers to percentile intervals, whereas Appendix E describes the same simultaneous rules as the primary analysis. Table 24 explicitly uses a percentile verdict for known-label ownership. Table 19 sometimes omits the random reference. Table 14 uses the original CORE sham after refitting the probe, although that sham is no longer necessarily a coordinate permutation of the refitted direction.

These are meaningful differences when the contribution is a benchmark with precisely defined acceptance criteria.

There is also a distinction between uncertainty conditional on fitted directions and uncertainty of the full procedure. Only 6/22 initially owned chest cells retain ownership under both available refits. Cell-wise bootstrap intervals in Figure 2 do not capture shared patients, related checkpoints, or training-direction uncertainty.

**Resolution:** Recompute claimed grade-preservation results with one common grading implementation, matching random and sham controls to each intervention construction. Clearly separate positive margins, clinical-comparison verdicts, and complete ownership. Define the upper-bound construction and error family explicitly. Report aggregate uncertainty using shared patient resampling across checkpoints, and assess training/projection variability on a representative subset. Provide family-balanced summaries alongside checkpoint-weighted counts.

### W7. The geometry of alternative directions is described inconsistently

**Locations:** §3.2; §A.7; Tables 16 and 17.

Appendix A.7 states:

> “The same lift applies to classifier normals, class-mean displacements, and patterns.”

This is not generally correct. For standardized projected features \(z=S^{-1}R^\top h\), a classifier coefficient lifts as a covector,
\[
R S^{-1}\beta,
\]
whereas a displacement \(u\) in standardized feature space has minimum-norm preimage
\[
R(R^\top R)^{-1}S u.
\]

Table 17 correctly implements the latter distinction, but Appendix A.7 still asserts a common lift and describes the Gaussian projection as having “near-orthonormal columns.” The reported condition numbers of 5.3–33.2 do not support treating its Gram matrix as uniformly close to identity.

Moreover, all main constructions share one 512-dimensional projection. Patient refits do not test sensitivity to that projection.

**Resolution:** Replace the common-lift argument with the two explicit transformations and make the geometrically appropriate displacement results the primary comparison for means and patterns. Report projection normalization and dimensions unambiguously. Add a bounded sensitivity experiment using multiple projection seeds and a higher-dimensional or unprojected probe on representative checkpoints.

## 4. Questions for the Authors

1. Which frozen manifest and protocol version generate the submitted results? What accounts for the Table 21 count mismatch and the dose-coverage differences between Figure 2c and Table 6?
2. How many stronger-competitor cells have both a meaningful positive own effect and a winning competitor that clears the random/sham reference? How many remain when an effect floor is applied to the competitor’s advantage?
3. Does the ownership deficit persist when clinical and non-clinical radiograph concepts use identical competitor families and reference rules, with comparable clean-answer capability?
4. Were any directions refitted using expert labels? If not, what evidence distinguishes a reader mismatch from directions distorted by report-label noise?
5. Which robustness results retain the complete primary grade after regenerating construction-specific shams and using the same simultaneous inference rule?
6. Can a controlled tower–reader crossover identify which component changes the clinical–COCO ownership gap?
