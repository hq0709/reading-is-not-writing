## 1. Summary

This paper studies whether directions learned by linear probes can selectively steer medical vision-language models when written into the visual representation consumed by the downstream reader. It separates readability, answerability, and “ownership,” with ownership requiring a concept’s direction to outperform random, sham, and competing clinical directions on its own question. Across the scored chest-radiograph cells, the authors report 56% readability, 62% answerability, and 12% ownership, compared with 88% ownership for COCO objects. A patient-disjoint replication confirms the motivating result that the Nodule direction moves the Effusion answer more than the Effusion direction does. Additional experiments examine direction construction, dose, prompts, numerical precision, and directions fitted to the model’s own answers. Comparisons between checkpoints sharing a vision tower provide compelling evidence that the downstream reader substantially affects intervention responses. The response-matrix benchmark is potentially valuable, but the alternative-direction implementation, incomplete numerical validation, and evidence connecting answer competition to clinical semantics require resolution before the strongest conclusions are established.

## 2. Strengths

1. **The clinical-competitor comparison is a useful evaluation contribution (§§3.2–3.4; Figure 4).** Comparing equally constructed, equally dosed directions on the same patients addresses an attribution question that random perturbations alone cannot answer. Reporting the complete question-by-direction matrix makes failure modes substantially more interpretable than reporting only a target-direction effect.

2. **The distinction between the three grades is operationally clear (§3.4; Table 1).** The paper does not automatically equate decodability with downstream use. The intersection analysis in §5.1—21 owned cells among 95 readable and answerable cells—is particularly informative because it examines the premise directly.

3. **The seed replication is persuasive for the specific empirical counterexample (§5.2; Table 19; Appendices B–C).** The locked dose, new patients, matched competitors, and paired uncertainty support the conclusion that the stronger Nodule effect on the Effusion question is reproducible. The appendix also distinguishes exploratory observations from subsequent confirmation experiments.

4. **The shared-tower comparisons provide strong evidence of reader dependence (§5.4; Figure 5; Table 12).** Holding features and write directions effectively fixed while observing large paired changes in ownership is a valuable experiment. The large Gemma differences are more informative than simply comparing unrelated model families.

5. **The benchmark examines both intervention strength and spillover (§5.1; Figures 4 and 7; Tables 9 and 18).** The combination of weak clinical diagonal effects and substantial effects on other questions provides richer evidence than a low ownership percentage alone.

6. **The study has substantial empirical breadth (§4; Tables 1–4).** Multiple model families, medical derivatives, two radiograph datasets, and a natural-image comparison make this a potentially useful resource. The consumed-token intervention design and explicit hook checks are also sensible engineering choices.

## 3. Weaknesses

### W1. The alternative-direction lifting rule appears mathematically incorrect for feature displacements

**Location:** Equation 2; §5.6; Appendix A.7; Tables 13 and 16.

> “The same lift carries classifier normals, class-mean displacements, and patterns alike.”

Classifier coefficients and feature displacements transform differently. This distinction is central to the paper’s own motivation from [Haufe et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24239590/).

Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(s)\), so the standardized features are \(z=A^{-1}R^\top h\). A classifier coefficient \(\beta\) correctly induces the representation-space normal \(RA^{-1}\beta\). However, a desired displacement \(u\) in standardized feature space requires, for full-column-rank \(R\), the minimum-norm lift
\[
\delta h=R(R^\top R)^{-1}Au.
\]
Even if \(R^\top R=I\), this uses \(A\), whereas the manuscript applies \(A^{-1}\). Normalizing the resulting vector does not remove this coordinatewise difference. Consequently, Table 13 does not presently establish that properly transformed class-mean or pattern directions fail.

There is also an immediate reporting problem:

> “They add a few chest cells and lose many natural-object cells, making them weaker handles everywhere.”

Table 13 reports **12/96 and 13/96 NIH cells owned by mean and pattern directions, versus 7/96 for logistic normals**.

**Required resolution:** Provide the implemented transformations and coordinate definitions; correct and rerun the displacement baselines; include native-feature means/patterns or justify the chosen projected-space reconstruction. Report the normalization and spectrum of \(R^\top R\). Correct the “weaker handles everywhere” sentence to reflect the actual comparisons.

For Table 16, also measure and match the realized perturbation norm. Mean-one token weights do not preserve total dose when token norms vary, and the claimed factor-of-two Frobenius norm requires an additional equal-energy assumption.

### W2. The claimed numerical validation substantially exceeds the reported experiment

**Location:** §3.5; §5.6; Tables 3, 11, and 17.

> “Rescoring the write grid with fp32 weights and batch size one changes \(W\) by at most 0.002 and leaves every verdict unchanged (Table 17).”

Table 17 contains **one Qwen2.5-VL-7B COCO block on 200 rows**. It reports agreement for two point-estimate comparisons, not the complete simultaneous-bound ownership verdict. This is particularly consequential because Table 3 reports batching deviations up to **1.84 logits**, including in Gemma, which supports the central reader experiment.

> “We declare batched-versus-single deviations above 0.25 and neutralise them by scoring every condition of a question in one fixed batch composition.”

Fixed composition improves pairing, but does not demonstrate that condition-dependent numerical errors cancel. The common baseline cancels algebraically in clinical contrasts; errors in the intervened forwards need not.

**Required resolution:** Run the numerical module on chest blocks covering the largest gate deviations, the key shared-tower comparisons, and ownership decisions closest to their thresholds. Use the same patients and recompute all reference comparisons and simultaneous bounds. Report changes in individual paired contrasts and final grades. Until these runs are available, the numerical-results paragraph must accurately identify the completed experiment and its endpoints.

### W3. The clinical-versus-COCO comparison does not yet isolate clinical specificity

**Location:** Abstract; §5.3; Appendix A.1; Tables 5, 9, 10, and 18.

> “The deficit is specific to clinical concepts.”

COCO establishes that this intervention procedure can succeed on natural objects. It does not isolate clinical semantics from annotation quality, visual difficulty, missing-label selection, or differences in downstream sensitivity. Table 9 already shows substantially larger random-write effects on chest images than on COCO.

The label handling makes this concern concrete. CheXpert Atelectasis has **84 positive, 2 negative, and 514 unknown test labels**, with only 86 known negatives in training. Known-label rescoring in Table 18 evaluates the same fitted directions on a selected subset; it cannot determine whether noisy or selectively observed training labels produced the deficit. Low marginal label correlations and competitor-match rates in Table 10 also do not exclude shared acquisition, severity, or annotation factors.

**Required resolution:** Add a radiologist-adjudicated, adequately supported chest subset for both direction fitting and evaluation. Compare clinical findings with nonclinical radiographic attributes in the same images, and compare against natural-object tasks matched for label support and clean/probe discrimination. Refit directions under documented label-handling alternatives and report paired ownership changes. These experiments would directly test the proposed clinical specificity.

### W4. Ownership needs validation as a semantic criterion, especially for the answer-direction result

**Location:** Abstract; §§3.3–3.4 and 5.6; Tables 14 and 18; Figure 7.

> “Only \(O_q>0\) shows that the label names the effective direction.”

Row dominance measures relative efficacy among the supplied directions. It does not itself establish semantic selectivity: a broadly disruptive direction can dominate its own row while strongly changing other answers. Conversely, a target-selective direction can lose its row to a stronger, broadly acting competitor. The column analyses help, but the paper reports them mainly as aggregate summaries rather than validating individual ownership decisions.

> “This answer direction \(a_q\) is a handle where the label direction is not.”

The answer-direction experiment is interesting, and Table 14 appropriately compares answer directions against other answer directions. However, predicting the model’s existing answer margin can recover an answer-interface or model-error direction. It does not independently establish clinical meaning. The rescue is also heterogeneous: Gemma 3 12B owns **0/6 CheXpert concepts with answer directions**, versus **1/6 with label directions**.

**Required resolution:** Report paired, per-concept rescue/loss identities together with column selectivity. Fit answer directions using one interface and test ownership under independently held-out paraphrases and reversed answer mappings. Include an independent semantic endpoint, such as a held-out clinical readout or controlled image-pair intervention, and distinguish changes in clinical evidence from changes in answer preference. Validate the ownership criterion on cases with known selective and shared intervention effects.

### W5. The shared-tower experiment identifies a reader effect, but does not establish the proposed exclusive attribution

**Location:** Abstract; §§3.1 and 5.4; Figure 5; Tables 12 and 18.

> “What a written direction does is decided by the reader of the representation—the connector and language model—not by the representation.”

The experiment convincingly demonstrates that changing the reader can change ownership while holding the representation approximately fixed. It does not establish that representation geometry is irrelevant, or that reader differences explain the clinical-versus-natural-image gap. Indeed, §3.1 correctly defines ownership as a property of the representation–reader pair.

The geometric evidence also requires explanation. Table 18 gives Lingshu 32B a covariance-weighted clinical alignment of **0.87 on CheXpert**, compared with **0.73 on COCO**. This is an important case for the claimed clinical misalignment mechanism.

**Required resolution:** Replay identical token-level clean and intervened tensors through the shared-tower readers at controlled precision. Add a complementary experiment varying representation construction with a fixed downstream reader—for example, controlled encoder adaptation with a frozen connector and language model. Test whether changes in answer-direction alignment predict changes in ownership, and explain the Lingshu counterexample. This would establish how the representation and reader contribute to the proposed mechanism.

### W6. The uncertainty analysis does not fully support the benchmark’s stability claims

**Location:** §§3.3–3.4 and 5.1; Figure 2; Tables 11 and 18; Appendix E.

> “Ownership is the least common and least stable of the three grades on the chest sets.”

The blockwise paired max-\(T\) design is appropriate for the 30 fixed clinical contrasts. However, its uncertainty is conditional on the fitted directions, projection, and selected reference family. Table 11 shows that only **6/10 owned NIH cells retain even a positive ownership margin under both refits**; retaining the full ownership grade is a stronger requirement.

Several additional details affect reproducibility: the simultaneous upper-bound construction for “stronger competitor” is not explicit; some robustness tables use percentile verdicts despite describing the “same rules”; and Figure 2 bootstraps cells that share patients, concepts, towers, and model lineage. The latter intervals are labeled descriptive, but they should not be interpreted as uncertainty over independent model–concept replications.

**Required resolution:** Report full-grade transition tables across refits and projections. State the exact upper- and lower-bound algorithms and the comparison family each controls. Recompute robustness checks using the original grade, alongside any alternative interval. For aggregate rates, use paired patient resampling and report sensitivity to family-level weighting. State how undefined AUROC bootstrap samples and extremely small class counts are handled.

### W7. Coverage and reproducibility claims conflict with the supplied tables

**Location:** Introduction; §4; §5.6; Appendices A.1–A.4; Tables 1, 9, 11, 15, and 17; Reproducibility statement.

> “Every block reports dose response, refit stability, a second locus, label-shift controls, and prompt-template contrasts.”

Table 9 has no CheXpert results for these modules; Appendix A.4 describes CheXpert coverage largely as write matrix plus calibration, while Table 11 suggests limited additional coverage.

> “We grade Llama 3.2 Vision 11B on its eligible templates.”

Table 1 instead reports an incomplete NIH matrix and ineligible CheXpert/COCO entries. Thus, 21 checkpoints are listed, but only 20 contribute scored ownership results.

> “Enlarging the clinical family … creates no ownership.”

This is guaranteed by the definition: adding competitors cannot increase the target-minus-maximum margin. Table 15 is useful as a retention test, but cannot serve as independent evidence against an alternative explanation.

The supplied text also states that the benchmark is released and registered without providing an accessible artifact identifier, protocol hash, or registration record.

**Required resolution:** Add a checkpoint × dataset × module coverage table generated from the artifact, with explicit status and denominator fields. Complete the promised eligible-template evaluation or accurately document its status. Identify which robustness analyses were registered versus added later. Provide an anonymous artifact link, immutable protocol/code identifiers, and a table-generation manifest. Describe Table 15 as ownership retention under added competitors.

## 4. Questions for the Authors

1. Does the Table 13 implementation apply \(R\operatorname{diag}(1/s)u\) to standardized class-mean and pattern displacements? What happens after correcting the displacement transformation?
2. Which chest blocks have completed full-forward fp32 and singleton-batch validation, including the complete ownership decision?
3. Which cohort supplies the readability and answerability AUROCs: calibration or test? How are low-support labels and bootstrap samples containing only one class handled?
4. How are uncertain and blank labels handled when residualizing against other findings and computing pairwise label correlations?
5. Does answer-direction ownership transfer to templates and answer mappings excluded from fitting? Which individual clinical cells are rescued or lost?
6. Are the shared-tower comparisons matched at the complete token-tensor level, including the actual perturbation, or only at pooled-feature and direction level?
7. What are the final Llama eligible-template results, and where can reviewers inspect the frozen protocol and actual module coverage?
