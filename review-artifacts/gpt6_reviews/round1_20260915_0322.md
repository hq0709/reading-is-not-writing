# ICLR Review

## 1. Summary

This paper studies whether directions that linearly decode clinical findings from a vision-language model also provide concept-specific control over its answers. It introduces three grades—readable, answerable, and owned—and defines ownership through competition between a concept’s probe direction, other clinical directions, random directions, and a permutation sham. The reported campaign covers 21 checkpoints from nine families, two chest-radiograph datasets, and a COCO object control, with clinical ownership reported at 12% versus 88% for objects. A patient-disjoint replication strengthens the motivating observation that a Nodule direction changes the Effusion answer more than the Effusion direction itself. Shared-tower comparisons and directions fitted to model answers provide additional evidence about how downstream readers respond to visual representations. The evaluation addresses an important weakness in steering studies, and its paired write matrices are potentially useful benchmark artifacts. However, conflicting result counts, incomplete validation of ownership as semantic control, and unresolved differences between the clinical and natural-image conditions currently prevent the strongest conclusions from being established.

## 2. Strengths

1. **The clinical-competitor comparison is useful and clearly motivated (§§2–3; Figure 4).** Comparing equally constructed clinical directions on the same question, images, locus, and dose provides information that random-direction controls alone cannot supply. Publishing the full write matrix makes competing explanations inspectable.

2. **The seed result has a meaningful independent replication (§5.2; Table 17; Appendices B–C).** The locked-dose Effusion result reproduces on new patients, including a negative ownership interval well separated from zero. The explicit distinction between initial discovery and subsequent registered confirmation is a methodological strength.

3. **The shared-tower comparisons provide unusually direct evidence of reader dependence (§5.4; Figure 5; Table 12).** Holding visual features and write directions approximately or exactly fixed while changing the downstream model is more informative than comparing unrelated checkpoints. The paired patient analysis appropriately exploits this design.

4. **The study goes beyond a single steering construction (§5.6; Tables 11–16).** Alternative directions, refits, connector interventions, token weighting, answer directions, and prompt variants address several plausible explanations. The answer-direction experiment is particularly promising as a constructive diagnostic.

5. **Several reported analyses expose limitations of the results themselves (§5.1; Table 11; Appendix C.3).** The effect-floor analysis, limited refit stability of owned NIH cells, and unsuccessful Mass confirmation are valuable disclosures. These results help distinguish a reproducible intervention effect from reliable semantic attribution.

## 3. Weaknesses

### W1. The headline results are not internally reconstructable

**Locations:** §4; §5.1; Table 1; Figure 2; Tables 2, 3, 6, 7, 10, and 13; Appendices A.3–A.4.

The statement that “every table and figure in the paper is regenerated from those files” is difficult to reconcile with several substantive discrepancies:

- Table 1’s ownership denominators sum to **126 + 108 + 114 = 348 cells**, whereas the paper reports **342**. Table 10 instead uses **120 + 108 + 114 = 342**.
- Figure 2 reports **19/114** owned CheXpert cells, while Table 1 and Table 7 report **17/108**. This changes both the dataset result and the pooled clinical numerator.
- Tables 2–3 and Appendix A.3 declare Llama 3.2 Vision’s yes/no templates ineligible on every dataset, yet Tables 1 and 6 report NIH answer/ownership results for it. If these use an eligible letter interface, their inclusion in a primary yes/no leaderboard needs an explicit rule.
- Appendix A.4 says InternVL3.5-38B is complete on all datasets, while its CheXpert entry is absent from Tables 1 and 7.
- §5.6 describes 42 alternative-direction blocks with 84 cells per dataset; Table 13 reports 46 blocks with denominators of 90, 90, and 96.

These could reflect different analysis snapshots, but the submission does not identify an authoritative one.

**Resolution:** Provide one machine-readable checkpoint–dataset–template–module manifest containing eligibility, completion, sample counts, and verdicts. Regenerate every aggregate from that manifest, identify the precise analysis commit, and explain each exclusion. The abstract’s percentages should be recomputed only after this reconciliation.

### W2. Clinical specificity remains confounded with label quality, task difficulty, and evaluation support

**Locations:** Abstract; §4; §5.3; Table 5; Appendix A.1.

The conclusion “The deficit is specific to clinical concepts” requires a better-controlled comparison. COCO uses instance annotations and has mean clean-answer AUROC around 0.96; the chest datasets use report-derived labels and have substantially lower answer AUROC. These conditions differ in annotation reliability, visual difficulty, missingness, and model competence.

Table 5 exposes a particularly important issue: CheXpert Atelectasis has **84 positive, two negative, and 514 unknown test rows**. It cannot satisfy the readable grade’s ten-negatives requirement, and its answer AUROC has extremely limited negative support. Ordinary bootstrap samples can contain no negatives. Furthermore, readability and answerability exclude unknown rows, while ownership includes all 600 rows, so the three grades can describe substantially different patient subsets.

Table 5’s NIH training counts also total **18,212**, rather than the 20,000 training rows specified in §4; 18,212 is the seed-study training count.

**Resolution:** Reconcile the training manifest and report effective sample sizes and undefined-bootstrap handling per concept. Add a prespecified clinical subset with independently adjudicated labels and adequate class support, including sufficiently reliable training labels for the tested directions. Compare clinical and natural-image ownership after matching or stratifying by probe AUROC, clean-answer competence, prevalence, and baseline confidence. Repeat the write analysis on known-label and adjudicated subsets while retaining the full-cohort result.

### W3. Ownership has not been validated as a test of whether a direction is a semantic handle

**Locations:** §1; §§3.3–3.4; §§5.1 and 5.6; Figure 7.

I object to the inference:

> “A concept with a readable but unowned probe direction is one the model can be read for but not written with.”

Ownership measures relative response strength. It does not, by itself, establish semantic specificity. For example, a direction that increases only its own answer by 0.2 fails ownership if another direction increases every answer by 0.3. The first direction still provides selective control. Row dominance and the distribution of a write across questions measure different properties.

The sentence “The deficit lies in attribution rather than efficacy” also conflicts with the reported decomposition: only **41/228** chest cells meet the steering reference. Thus, **187/228** fail that efficacy criterion before clinical competition determines ownership. The reported 14/41 failures after meeting the reference and 19/91 owned readable-and-answerable cells are useful, but they do not establish which mechanism explains the overall gap.

**Resolution:** Validate ownership using controlled examples containing known concept-specific interventions and generic answer-bias interventions. Report the joint distribution of meaningful own effects, reference passage, stronger competitors, unresolved contrasts, and column-wise selectivity. Extend the existing conditional analyses to compare semantic competition at matched write strength. Demonstrate that the clinical deficit persists on independent semantic endpoints, such as held-out question formulations and separate target-versus-unrelated-finding assessments, with signed interventions and patient strata reported explicitly.

### W4. The answer-direction experiment establishes answer sensitivity more directly than clinical semantics

**Locations:** Abstract; §5.6, “The direction the reader responds to”; Table 14; §7.

The statements

> “This answer direction \(a_q\) is a handle where the label direction is not.”

and

> “On chest radiographs, the model answers along a direction the probe does not read.”

require additional evidence. Regressing the model’s own answer margin deliberately targets its response behavior. Successful intervention along that fitted direction is informative, but the fitted signal could include question-specific biases, acquisition cues, or general abnormality.

Table 14 appropriately compares answer directions against other answer directions, which makes the result stronger than an unmatched oracle comparison. Nevertheless, its clinical evidence covers only two checkpoints. Raw cosine also does not establish functional separation: two directions with low Euclidean cosine can produce correlated scores under an anisotropic feature distribution. Conversely, COCO cosines of 0.54–0.64 do not establish that the directions “coincide.”

**Resolution:** Report the answer direction’s clinical-label AUROC, its score correlation with the label probe, covariance-aware alignment, and refit uncertainty. Test transfer to question wordings and answer encodings excluded from answer-direction fitting. Add prespecified readers with different ownership and answerability profiles, and compare against directions fitted to generic yes propensity and general abnormality.

### W5. The shared-tower experiment does not yet establish the claimed exclusion of representation effects

**Locations:** Abstract; §3.1; §5.4; Figure 5; Table 12.

The strongest formulation is:

> “What a written direction does is decided by the reader of the representation—the connector and language model—not by the representation.”

The shared-tower experiment establishes that changing the reader can change ownership while holding the representation fixed. It does not measure what happens when the representation changes while the reader is held fixed. Indeed, §3.1 correctly defines ownership as a property of the representation–reader pair.

There is also a distinction between bit-identical features and vectors whose cosines exceed 0.9987. Directional proximity alone does not bound downstream response differences.

**Resolution:** Feed exactly the same cached clean and edited tensors to the compared readers. Add controlled tower/connector/decoder exchanges where architectures permit, including a representation comparison with a fixed reader and a connector-versus-decoder decomposition. Report both full write matrices and ownership changes. These experiments would directly quantify the reader contribution and test the competing representation explanation.

### W6. The alternative-direction and token-weighting controls leave important implementation questions open

**Locations:** §§3.1–3.2 and 5.6; Tables 13 and 16.

The statement “No measurement alternative explains the deficit” is stronger than the reported controls establish.

All five direction constructions use the same fixed 512-dimensional projection and scaling. Their agreement therefore does not rule out a restriction imposed by that shared feature transformation. Moreover, mapping a classifier normal back to model space and mapping a feature displacement back are different operations. If \(x=Ah\), the normal pulls back as \(A^\top\beta\); a class-mean displacement or Haufe pattern requires its own mapping. The manuscript does not specify this sufficiently to assess the alternatives.

Table 16 also describes mean-one token weights as the “same total dose.” That does not preserve perturbation energy. With equal token norms, writing to one quarter of tokens at four times the weight doubles the intervention’s Frobenius norm.

**Resolution:** Specify every construction and its lift mathematically. Include native-space class-mean and covariance-pattern baselines, a native-space probe, and several projection seeds on a representative subset. Normalize token-weighted interventions to equal per-image perturbation norm and apply equivalent weighting rules to controls. Report both relative activation displacement and resulting downstream displacement across dose.

### W7. The statistical specification and robustness summaries need correction

**Locations:** §§3.3–3.4 and 5.6; Figure 2; Table 11; Appendices A.7 and E.

The sentence “every owned cell stays owned” on the logit scale is contradicted by Table 11. NIH has ten probability-owned cells but only eight owned on both scales; COCO has 100 probability-owned cells but 99 owned on both. Thus, three probability-owned cells do not retain ownership.

There are additional specification gaps:

- Table 11 mentions percentile intervals for logit-scale ownership, whereas the primary grade uses simultaneous bounds on fixed contrasts. Changing both scale and inference procedure does not isolate scale robustness.
- Appendix E specifies the seed study’s 400-patient, 5,000-draw analysis, while the main campaign uses 600 rows and 2,000 draws. The campaign’s exact implementation and simultaneous upper-bound construction are not provided there.
- Figure 2 bootstraps cells even though checkpoints share patients, concepts, and sometimes vision towers. Those intervals do not capture the dependence relevant to the headline proportions.
- §3.2 describes random labels drawn within recurring types, while Appendix B assigns random labels to types. These are different control tasks and need separate, explicit algorithms.

**Resolution:** Supply a campaign-specific inference algorithm, use identical decision rules for probability and logit comparisons, and publish a cell-level verdict transition table. Compute aggregate uncertainty through joint patient resampling across checkpoints and report sensitivity to family weighting and probe refits. Specify the control-label generation and handling of insufficient-class bootstrap samples.

### W8. Failed numerical gates are declared neutralized without sufficient supporting evidence

**Locations:** §3.5; Table 3; Appendices A.3 and A.7; Table 11.

The claim

> “We declare batched-versus-single deviations above 0.25 and neutralise them by scoring every condition of a question in one fixed batch composition.”

does not follow from fixed batch composition alone. That design controls batching differences between conditions, but input-dependent numerical errors can still change under intervention. Recomputing the final answer logits in fp32 checks the output projection, not numerical error accumulated through the vision tower and language model.

The reported margin-sign agreement in 55/61 blocks also does not establish unchanged three-way verdicts. A block can move between “stronger competitor” and “unresolved” without containing an owned cell.

**Resolution:** Re-evaluate the affected comparisons under a numerically stable reference configuration and report changes in own effects, pairwise contrasts, and all three verdict categories. For small margins, compare numerical discrepancies directly with confidence-bound distances from zero. Publish the acceptance thresholds and the exact post-failure rule in the frozen protocol.

## 4. Questions for the Authors

1. Which manifest and analysis commit produce the authoritative 342-cell result, and how are Llama’s NIH results and InternVL3.5-38B’s CheXpert results handled?
2. Are the NIH probe-training rows the new 20,000-row cohort or the seed study’s 18,212 rows? Can patient-disjointness across every training, calibration, and intervention cohort be verified from the manifests?
3. How are CheXpert Atelectasis AUROCs and bootstrap draws with no negatives handled? What happens to the clinical–COCO gap on adequately supported, known-label subsets?
4. How are the difference-of-means and Haufe-pattern vectors lifted from scaled projected features into model space?
5. Do answer directions predict independently assessed clinical labels and transfer to question interfaces excluded from their fitting?
6. What roles do the 400 calibration rows play in the main campaign? Which decisions, including any eligibility or parameter choices, depend on them?
7. Where can reviewers access the anonymized artifact, frozen protocol, registration timestamp, and per-image outcomes?

## 6. Prioritized Fix List

### P0 — Required to establish the main result

- **§4; §5.1; Table 1; Figure 2; Tables 6–13; Appendices A.3–A.4:** Build one authoritative coverage/verdict manifest and regenerate all counts, percentages, figures, and alternative-direction summaries. Resolve template eligibility and missing-block discrepancies explicitly.
- **Table 5; Appendix A.1; §5.3:** Verify cohort provenance and label support. Add an adequately supported, adjudicated clinical comparison and match or stratify the COCO comparison by competence and task difficulty.
- **§§3.3–3.5; Table 11; Appendix E:** Specify the campaign inference procedure, correct the scale-transition claims, and verify affected verdicts under a stable numerical reference configuration.

### P1 — Required to substantiate the explanatory claims

- **§§3.3–3.4; §§5.1 and 5.6; Figure 7:** Validate ownership against known selective and generic interventions. Separate weak efficacy, semantic competition, and unresolved effects; demonstrate the clinical deficit on independent specificity measures.
- **§5.6; Table 14:** Test answer directions against clinical labels, unseen question interfaces, generic-answer controls, and additional prespecified readers. Add covariance-aware alignment and refit uncertainty.
- **§5.4; Figure 5; Table 12:** Use identical cached tensors and controlled component exchanges to quantify representation, connector, and decoder contributions.
- **§3.2; Tables 13 and 16:** Document correct vector mappings; add native-space and projection-seed controls; equalize perturbation norm across token-weighting variants.

### P2 — Improve clarity and reproducibility

- **§5.6; Table 9:** Correct “relabelling test rows within type shifts the answer.” The defined label gap compares intervention effects between positive and negative rows. If a separate relabelling experiment exists, specify its intervention and estimator.
- **§3.1; Table 2; Appendix D; reproducibility statement:** Provide exact consumed module paths, complete templates, token-scoring rules, adapter configurations, and an anonymized artifact link. Correct Appendix D.1’s row/column description to match \(W_{q,d}\), where questions are rows and directions are columns.
