Abstract
A linear probe reads a clinical concept from a vision-language model’s visual
representation. Writing that direction back into the same representation tests
whether it is a handle on the concept: it must move the concept’s own answer
more than every other direction constructed the same way. On chest radiographs,
it almost never is. At the visual block the language model consumes, we define
three criteria: whether a concept is readable (probe AUROC above matched
random-label controls), answerable (the model’s own yes/no answer beats chance),
and owned (a fixed-dose write along the probe direction moves the concept’s answer
more than the 95th percentile of 119 random directions, a coordinate-permutation
sham, and every competing clinical direction, with simultaneous max-𝑇 bounds).
Across 21 checkpoints from nine model families (3B to 72B) on NIH ChestX-ray14
and CheXpert Plus, clinical concepts are readable in 56% of checkpoint–concept
cells, answerable in 61%, and owned in only 12%. In two thirds of the cells, a
competing clinical direction moves the concept’s answer more than its own direction
does. The seed result reproduces on new patients (ownership −0.071, 95% CI
[−0.080, −0.062]). On COCO objects, the same procedure owns 88% of cells, with
three quarters of every write landing on its own question. The write mechanism is
intact. The deficit is specific to clinical concepts. The handle the reader responds
to exists in the same representation: a direction fitted to the model’s own answers
is owned where the probe direction is not. On chest radiographs, this answer
direction is nearly orthogonal to the probe direction (cosine 0.1 against 0.6 on
natural images). Gemma 3 and MedGemma checkpoints of different sizes share
one vision tower and therefore have identical probes and write vectors. Yet they
own different concepts. What a written direction does is decided by the reader of
the representation—the connector and language model—not by the representation.
The protocol, acceptance gates, and per-checkpoint leaderboard form a reusable
benchmark for concept writability in medical vision-language models.

Introduction

Linear probes are the standard tool for asking what a vision-language model (VLM) represents: fit a
classifier to a chosen layer’s activations and read out a concept (Alain & Bengio, 2017; Belinkov,
2022). In medical imaging, probes often succeed—clinical findings such as pleural effusion or
cardiomegaly are linearly decodable from the visual representation of general-purpose and medical
VLMs (Zhu et al., 2026; Pedersen et al., 2026). Activation steering promises the converse operation:
write the probe direction back into the representation and move the model’s behaviour (Turner et al.,
2023; Panickssery et al., 2024). Together, these operations would make a probe direction a handle on
the concept: read it to detect, write it to control. This paper asks whether that handle exists.
Showing that steering “works” does not answer the question. A write can change an answer for
reasons unrelated to the concept’s label. Any perturbation of matched size can push a yes/no answer.
A direction fitted for one finding can carry a generic abnormality signal shared with other findings
(Pahde et al., 2025). Random and sham directions test the first possibility. Testing the second requires
a comparison steering studies rarely make: write every competing clinical direction at the same locus
and dose in the same patients, then ask whether the concept’s own direction moves the concept’s own

Background and Related Work

Probing and its controls. Linear classifiers have long been used to track information across network
depth (Alain & Bengio, 2017). A probe score reflects both the representation and the learner fitted to
it. This motivates matched-capacity probes and control tasks (Hewitt & Liang, 2019). The probing
literature distinguishes information a readout can recover from information the model uses (Belinkov,
2022; Ravichander et al., 2021). Causal erasure pursues the complementary test (Ravfogel et al., 2020;
Elazar et al., 2021). Our readability grade adopts the control-task idea: it measures selectivity against
random-label probes of equal capacity fitted within recurring image types. Depth-wise probing of
medical multimodal models has mapped classification information across towers, connectors, and
language layers (Zhu et al., 2026). Layer-wise probes of MedCLIP expose acquisition and device
shortcuts (Pedersen et al., 2026). Neither intervenes on the decoded clinical direction at the same
locus.
Steering with probe normals. Activation engineering changes intermediate representations at
inference time and measures the resulting output changes (Turner et al., 2023; Panickssery et al.,
2024). A probe normal is an appealing intervention because it supplies a label and a signed vector
from the same representation. Separability-trained concept vectors can absorb distractor signals
and diverge from the concept they are meant to model (Pahde et al., 2025). A classifier’s weight
vector is a filter, not the pattern it detects (Haufe et al., 2014). Mass-mean directions steer truth
judgements in language models more effectively than logistic normals (Marks & Tegmark, 2024).
Representation-level surgery on VLM image tokens changes downstream answers (Rajaram et al.,
2025). Random-direction and sham controls test generic sensitivity and coordinate dependence. They
do not test whether an equally constructed direction with a different label would move the same answer
more. Ownership adds this comparison and evaluates it in the forward path at the consumed locus,
using each token’s norm as the dose scale. It scores difference-of-means, pattern, orthogonalised, and
residualised directions alongside the logistic normal (Section 5.6).

Acceptance gates

Seven checks run on 16 held-out preflight rows at both loci before block scoring (Table 3): bitwise
determinism; identity at 𝛼 = 0; reach; batched-versus-single agreement of the answer logits; imagefree semantic mapping of every template; throughput; and fp32 recomputation of the answer logits.

mapping are INELIGIBLE and closed as such. The block’s scorecard records every declared

Results
Reading is not writing

Table 1 reports scores for every checkpoint on every dataset. Figure 2 shows every concept cell across
the three grades. Both chest datasets show the same pattern. Clinical concepts are readable in 134
of 240 cells (56%): probe selectivity reaches 0.1–0.3 over matched random-label controls for most
findings in most families. The models also answer: 140 of the 228 cells with a scored write matrix
(61%) have a clean-answer AUROC whose lower bound exceeds chance. Among these answerable
cells, 91 are also readable. Yet only 27 cells (12%) are owned. In 150 cells (66%), the verdict is
stronger competitor—a direction fitted for another finding moves the concept’s answer more than its
own direction does. Readability and answerability do not suffice for writing: of the 91 readable and
answerable cells, 19 are owned (21%), against 8 of the remaining 137 (6%). Ownership is the least
common and least stable of the three grades on the chest sets. Applying an effect floor of 0.05 to both
𝑊𝑞,𝑞 and 𝑂 𝑞 leaves 18 of the 27 owned chest cells (8% of 228), against 78 of the 100 owned COCO
cells (68%). Refitting the probes on resampled patients leaves 6 of the 10 owned NIH cells with
𝑂 𝑞 > 0, against 94 of 100 on COCO. Clinical writes are not selective on the chest sets. Of the answer
change a clinical write produces across the six questions, 24% lands on its own question on NIH and
22% on CheXpert, against 79% on COCO (Figure 7). We call this pattern the attribution deficit. The
model exposes the concept to a probe. The probe’s direction is not a handle on the concept. Written
back, it moves other findings’ answers as much as its own.

6

Implications

For steering, model building, and clinical evaluation. A probe direction that reads a clinical
concept is not a handle on it. Probe accuracy and answer accuracy, the two quantities most often
reported for medical VLMs, do not establish whether a handle exists: both are high in this grid,
yet ownership is 12%. A steering study that beats random and sham controls shows that the write
reaches the answer, not that the label names the direction. At the same locus and dose in the same
patients, a direction fitted for another finding moves the same answer as much or more in two thirds of
clinical cells. The matched-competitor test is cheap once competitors’ directions exist. Studies using
probe normals as interventions should report the write matrix 𝑊. They should compare ownership
𝑂 𝑞 across layers, doses, and models. Every clinical write needs a natural-image control. The same
write vector produces different ownership in different language models. A vision tower that exposes a
finding linearly therefore does not make its direction a handle. Ownership rises with the reader in
some families and not in others (Gemma 3, InternVL3.5).

7

Conclusion

Reading a clinical concept from a vision-language model’s visual representation does not make its
probe direction a handle. Across 21 checkpoints and two chest-radiograph datasets, concepts are
readable in 56% and answerable in 61% of cells but owned in 12%. The same procedure owns 88%
of natural-object cells. The direction the model answers along is a handle and is nearly orthogonal to
the probe direction on chest radiographs. Checkpoints sharing a vision tower still differ in what they
own, so the reader of the representation decides ownership.

Ethics statement
The study uses three public datasets under their published terms: NIH ChestX-ray14 (NIH Clinical
Center), CheXpert Plus (Stanford AIMI, accessed through its data-use agreement), and COCO

A

Campaign details

This appendix lists the checkpoints and their consumed loci, the acceptance decisions, the coverage of
the campaign, and the per-concept results of every completed block.
A.1

Labels, registration, and artifact

Labels. Concept labels are the report-derived labels shipped with each dataset (NIH ChestXray14 finding labels; the CheXpert labeler output distributed with CheXpert Plus; COCO instance
annotations). A row is positive when the label is 1, negative when it is 0, and unknown when the
labeler returns uncertain or blank; unknown rows are excluded from probe fitting, selectivity, and
answer AUROC, and are written and scored like every other row. Cohorts are fixed manifests drawn
with recorded seeds, patient-disjoint between training and test and disjoint from every seed-study
cohort; per-concept counts are in Table 5.
Registration. The protocol was frozen before any block of the grid was scored: the loci, the
projection and probe settings, the dose, the reference family, the six templates, the module set,
the seven gates, and the three decision rules are those of the registered protocol file and its code
commit, and the seed study’s crossover and confirmation analyses were registered before they
were run (Appendix B). Everything reported per block is produced by one analysis pass from the
packaged outcomes; the leaderboard, the write-flow share, and the per-image examples are descriptive
summaries of those outputs added after the grid was complete.
Artifact. The release contains the evaluation code and adapters, the registered protocol file, the
cohort manifests with their seeds, the fitted directions, the per-image outcomes of every module (write,
dose, refit, locus, and template rows), every block’s scorecard and coverage file, and the rendered
prompts; a new checkpoint is added with one adapter that names its consumed block and passes the
gates.

A.3

Acceptance decisions

All 57 completed blocks pass checks (A), (B), (C), and (G) with zero determinism error, zero change
at 𝛼 = 0, and fp32-versus-model logit differences below 0.13. Check (D) exceeds the 0.25-logit
tolerance for the Qwen3-VL, Gemma 3, MedGemma, InternVL, and Llama families (up to 1.84
logits for Gemma 3 12B); these are recorded as declared deviations and neutralised by the fixed batch
composition. Check (E) fails the A/B templates for LLaVA-1.5-7B and LLaVA-Med, the B-mapped
templates for Qwen2.5-VL-3B and LLaVA-1.5-13B, and the yes/no and A-mapped templates for
Llama 3.2 Vision 11B on all datasets; the affected cells are recorded as ineligible. Two checkpoints
answer “yes” to nearly every chest question (Gemma 3 4B: clean 𝑃(yes) > 0.99 on 94–97% of rows),
which the answerability grade records; three (LLaVA-1.5 7B/13B, LLaVA-Med) answer at chance.

A.4

Coverage and cost

Nineteen checkpoints are complete on all three datasets with every module (the write matrix,
calibration, the five additional templates, the dose sweep, the two refits, and the connector locus on
NIH and COCO; the write matrix and calibration on CheXpert); Llama 3.2 Vision 11B is complete
with its yes/no modules ineligible; Qwen2.5-VL-72B is complete on NIH; the 72B COCO/CheXpert
blocks and Llama 3.2 Vision 90B were not run (compute budget), and InternVL3.5-38B is complete
on all three datasets. The campaign consumed about 3,000 A100-hours; a chest block of a 7B
checkpoint takes about ten GPU-hours.

Robustness of the deficit

Direction and label geometry. Neither correlation among the six directions nor co-occurrence of
the six report labels explains why a competing direction wins (Table 10). On NIH, the six directions are
as orthogonal as random vectors (mean off-diagonal cosine 0.019 against 0.023 for random pairs), and
the labels are nearly uncorrelated (mean 𝜙 = 0.026). Yet NIH shows the same off-diagonal dominance
as CheXpert, where directions and labels are correlated (mean cosine 0.24; Effusion–Consolidation
0.51, 𝜙 = 0.74). The own direction wins in 26% of NIH and 29% of CheXpert cells. The strongest
competitor matches the most similar direction at chance rate (19–23%) and the most co-occurring
label at chance rate (17–25%). Across the 1,140 chest off-diagonal cells, the competitor’s advantage
𝑊𝑞,𝑑 − 𝑊𝑞,𝑞 is uncorrelated with cosine (Spearman 0.05, 𝑝 = 0.11) and with 𝜙 (0.003). Removing
each cell’s strongest competitor does not restore ownership: 126 of 228 chest cells (55%) have at least
two competitors above the own write. The natural-image families with the highest co-occurrence
(person with bicycle, 𝜙 = 0.14; person with dog; chair with bottle) retain ownership in 98–100% of
cells when the competitor set is restricted to them. In the seed cell, Nodule is the least similar of the
five directions to Effusion (cosine −0.06). It still moves the Effusion answer more than Effusion’s
own direction (0.262 against 0.190). The most similar direction, Atelectasis (cosine 0.49), moves it
by 0.083.
Scale, saturation, refits, and numerics. The grades hold on the logit scale: ownership recomputed
from the margin ℓyes − ℓno agrees with the probability-scale verdict in 335 of 342 cells, and every
owned cell stays owned (Table 11). Saturation runs counter to a ceiling explanation: COCO blocks
have far more rows at 𝑃(yes) > 0.99 or < 0.01 than chest blocks (median block share 0.82 against
0.18 and 0.32). Restricting every cell to its unsaturated rows preserves 𝑂 𝑞 > 0 in all 27 owned chest
cells and in 32 of the 191 others. The same pattern holds in the clean-No, clean-Yes, label-present,
and label-absent strata. Refitting the probes on resampled training patients changes 𝑂 𝑞 by a median
of 0.03. The bf16 batch effects declared at the gates (candidate-logit differences of 0.07–1.84) leave
the verdicts unchanged: the margin sign agrees in 55 of 61 blocks, and the six disagreeing blocks
contain no owned cell. Re-scoring the clean baseline inside a second module gives bitwise agreement
in 100% of rows for 22 of 33 blocks and in 75% for the rest, with |Δ𝑃(yes)| > 0.1 in at most 1.9%
of rows. Writes at the connector locus are an order of magnitude smaller than at the primary locus
(median |𝑊𝑞,𝑞 | 0.009 against 0.046 on NIH and 0.008 against 0.270 on COCO).

connector, and batch statistics behind Section 5.6, Table 12 the paired shared-tower differences behind
Section 5.4, and Tables 13–16 the alternative directions, the answer-direction oracle, the extended

C.2

Semantic competition changes attribution, not efficacy

The fixed clinical family provides a harder identification test than non-semantic controls. Nodule
changes the Effusion answer by 0.2519 and realizes the point-estimate maximum among the five
alternatives. The Effusion ownership contrast 𝑂 Effusion is −0.0573. The registered fixed-family
endpoint recomputes the maximum inside each of 5,000 patient-bootstrap draws, giving a two-sided
95% CI of [−0.0694, −0.0450] and a one-sided 95% lower bound of −0.0678. The entire interval
is below zero. Descriptively, all five alternative clinical normals move the Effusion endpoint in the
same positive direction; Effusion exceeds four by 0.1172–0.1778, while Nodule alone has a larger
point estimate. This shared-sign pattern motivates an answer-sensitive-alias hypothesis but cannot
distinguish it from question-specific ownership.
Qwen Effusion therefore remains a reproducible direction that changes the answer beyond random and
sham perturbations, while its specific semantic attribution fails. The familywise endpoint establishes
that at least one fixed clinical alternative is stronger; Nodule is the point-estimate maximizer, not a
separately familywise-identified semantic owner.
C.3

Wording changes clinical competition within the answer interface

The second finding concerns the conditions of clinical competition. The Qwen ownership pattern
persists in logit coordinates, so we test whether changing the answer interface changes a direction’s
relative advantage.
In the registered crossover, Atelectasis, Pneumothorax, Mass, and Nodule pass independent capability
calibration. On these four questions, mapping-even minus mapping-odd response energy is −2.1786
(95% CI [−2.4061, −1.9810]): effects are larger in the component that reverses raw A-minus-B sign
when the finding-to-letter mapping is exchanged. Yet all 20 random directions and the sham are
also mapping-odd dominant. Aggregate mapping response therefore does not distinguish the clinical
normals from generic perturbations.
The crossover identifies Mass as a prompt-conditioned candidate: its clinical margin changes from
−0.7181 under the original yes/no prompt to 0.2825 and 0.2900 under A-present and B-present,
respectively (Table 19). A-present assigns A to presence and B to absence; B-present reverses

D.1

Prompts, Scores, and Notation
A common question and answer endpoint

At the first answer position, let ℓyes and ℓno denote the maximum log probabilities among the valid singletoken case and leading-space variants of each answer. The registered score is 𝑃(yes) = 𝜎(ℓyes − ℓno ),
where 𝜎 is the logistic sigmoid. The original Edema cell uses “pulmonary edema,” and the closure
experiment uses “consolidation” in the same template. Taking the best valid token variant for each
answer makes the endpoint insensitive to which listed capitalization or leading-space form receives
the highest score. This endpoint measures the relative support for yes versus no at the first answer
position; it does not measure the accuracy of a generated clinical report.
The shared interface makes the question–direction grid interpretable: changing a clinical direction
does not change the question being scored. Differences across columns still reflect different clinical
questions, so the ownership test compares directions within each column before drawing a conclusion
about the named concept.
