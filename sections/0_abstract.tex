A probe direction can reproducibly change a clinical answer without earning attribution to its training label. Random and sham controls test whether an intervention exceeds generic perturbations, but leave its advantage over other clinical directions untested. We study this distinction through semantic competition at fixed consumed visual loci in LLaVA-1.5-7B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B on NIH ChestX-ray14, holding direction construction, dose, patients, and endpoint constant within each comparison. Two Qwen findings separate intervention efficacy from label-specific attribution. First, locked-dose Effusion steering replicates on 400 independent patients beyond random p95 and sham, yet its effect is smaller than the strongest fixed clinical competitor's by $0.0573$ (ownership contrast $-0.0573$, 95\% CI $[-0.0694,-0.0450]$). Second, Mass outperforms all five clinical competitors on 200 independent patients under both \emph{show} A/B mappings, with positive simultaneous lower bounds, while both \emph{is} margins are negative. Paired wording analyses descriptively support this dependence. Larger effects among 119 random directions defeat both registered Mass specificity criteria. Diagnostic benefit is also unsupported: all confirmation AUROC-change intervals cross zero, and Brier error increases in every prompt condition. Semantic competition measures relative advantage within a fixed clinical family. These findings establish that successful steering can coexist with a clinical attribution deficit, and that clinical advantage can depend on wording.
