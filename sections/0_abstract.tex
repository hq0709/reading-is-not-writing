A linear probe reads a clinical concept out of a vision-language model's visual representation. Writing the probe direction back into the same representation is the test of whether that direction is a handle on the concept: whether it moves the concept's own answer more than every other direction constructed the same way. On chest radiographs it almost never is. We define a three-part evaluation at the visual block the language model consumes: whether a concept is \emph{readable} (probe AUROC above matched random-label controls), \emph{answerable} (the model's own yes/no answer beats chance), and \emph{owned} (a fixed-dose write along the probe direction moves the concept's answer more than the 95th percentile of 119 random directions, a coordinate-permutation sham, and every competing clinical direction, with simultaneous max-$T$ bounds). Across 21 checkpoints from nine model families (3B to 72B) on NIH ChestX-ray14 and CheXpert Plus, clinical concepts are readable in 56\% of checkpoint--concept cells and answerable in 61\%, but owned in 12\%: in two thirds of the cells a competing clinical direction moves the concept's answer more than the concept's own direction, and only a quarter of a clinical write's effect lands on the concept's own question. In the seed model, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Effusion steering reproduces beyond random and sham controls on new patients while the Nodule direction moves the Effusion answer more (ownership $-0.071$, 95\% CI $[-0.080,-0.062]$). The same procedure on COCO objects owns 88\% of cells, with three quarters of every write landing on its own question, so the write mechanism is intact and the deficit is specific to clinical concepts. Gemma~3 and MedGemma checkpoints of different sizes share one vision tower, so their probes and write vectors are identical, yet they own different concepts: what a written direction does is decided by the reader of the representation---the connector and language model---not by the representation. The protocol, its acceptance gates, and the per-checkpoint leaderboard form a reusable benchmark for concept writability in medical vision-language models.
