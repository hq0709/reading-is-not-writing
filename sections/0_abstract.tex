A linear probe reads a clinical concept from a vision-language model's visual representation. Writing the probe direction back into the same representation tests whether it is a handle on the concept: whether it moves the concept's own answer more than every other direction constructed the same way. On chest radiographs, it almost never is. We define three evaluation criteria at the visual block the language model consumes: whether a concept is \emph{readable} (probe AUROC above matched random-label controls), \emph{answerable} (the model's own yes/no answer beats chance), and \emph{owned} (a fixed-dose write along the probe direction moves the concept's answer more than the 95th percentile of 119 random directions, a coordinate-permutation sham, and every competing clinical direction, with simultaneous max-$T$ bounds). Across 21 checkpoints from nine model families (3B to 72B) on NIH ChestX-ray14 and CheXpert Plus, clinical concepts are readable in 56\% of checkpoint--concept cells and answerable in 61\%, but owned in 12\%. In two thirds of the cells, a competing clinical direction moves the concept's answer more than its own direction does. Only a quarter of a clinical write's effect lands on the concept's own question. The seed result reproduces on new patients (ownership $-0.071$, 95\% CI $[-0.080,-0.062]$). The same procedure on COCO objects owns 88\% of cells, with three quarters of every write landing on its own question. Thus, the write mechanism is intact and the deficit is specific to clinical concepts. The handle the reader responds to exists in the same representation: a direction fitted to the model's own answers is owned where the probe direction is not. On chest radiographs, it is nearly orthogonal to the probe direction (cosine $0.1$ against $0.6$ on natural images). Gemma~3 and MedGemma checkpoints of different sizes share one vision tower and therefore have identical probes and write vectors. Yet they own different concepts: what a written direction does is decided by the reader of the representation---the connector and language model---not by the representation. The protocol, acceptance gates, and per-checkpoint leaderboard form a reusable benchmark for concept writability in medical vision-language models.
