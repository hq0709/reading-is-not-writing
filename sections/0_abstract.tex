A linear probe reads a clinical concept out of a vision-language model's visual representation; writing the probe direction back into that representation is the natural test of whether the model uses it. We show that these two operations come apart. We define a three-part evaluation at the visual block the language model consumes: whether a concept is \emph{readable} (probe AUROC above matched random-label controls), \emph{answerable} (the model's own yes/no answer beats chance), and \emph{owned} (a fixed-dose write along the probe direction moves the concept's answer more than 119 random directions, a coordinate-permutation sham, and every competing clinical direction, with simultaneous max-$T$ bounds). Across 22 checkpoints from nine model families (3B to 90B) on NIH ChestX-ray14 and CheXpert Plus, clinical concepts are readable in 55\% of checkpoint--concept cells and answerable in 60\%, but owned in 12\%; in the seed model, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Effusion steering reproduces beyond random and sham controls on new patients while a competing direction moves its answer more (ownership $-0.071$, 95\% CI $[-0.080,-0.062]$). The same procedure on COCO objects owns 87\% of cells with effects up to $0.9$, so the write mechanism is intact and the deficit is specific to clinical concepts. Gemma~3 and MedGemma checkpoints of different sizes share one vision tower, so their probes and write vectors are byte-identical, yet ownership differs across sizes: writability is a property of the reader, not of the visual representation. The protocol, its acceptance gates, and the per-checkpoint leaderboard form a reusable benchmark for concept writability in medical vision-language models.
