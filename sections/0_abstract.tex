A linear probe gives a clinical concept both a score and a direction, inviting a causal interpretation when steering that direction changes a model's answer. We test the missing identification step: whether the named probe normal is more effective than equally scaled directions for other clinical findings. Our registered three-rung protocol aligns controlled decodability, generic perturbation selectivity, and clinical direction specificity at the exact final visual block consumed downstream by LLaVA-1.5-7B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B. Across three model--concept cells on NIH ChestX-ray14, controlled decoding is robust: AUROC ranges from 0.7742 to 0.8009, and every selectivity interval excludes zero. The two LLaVA probe normals establish availability but do not exceed matched random and coordinate-permutation controls. Qwen Effusion does, both in the original cohort and on 400 independent patients, where it changes mean $P(yes)$ by 0.1945. The decisive clinical comparison changes the result: Nodule steering moves the same Effusion answer by 0.2519, yielding a familywise Effusion-minus-maximum-unrelated margin of $-0.0573$ (95\% patient-bootstrap CI $[-0.0694,-0.0450]$). Random and sham controls can therefore establish a non-generic perturbation while leaving its clinical identity unresolved. Clinical-direction specificity is a distinct third rung for causal interpretation of decoded directions in medical vision-language models.
