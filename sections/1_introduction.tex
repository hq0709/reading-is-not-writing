\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Linear probes are the standard tool for asking what a vision-language model (VLM) represents: fit a classifier to a chosen layer's activations and read out a concept \citep{alain2017understanding,belinkov2022probing}. In medical imaging, probes often succeed---clinical findings such as pleural effusion or cardiomegaly are linearly decodable from the visual representation of general-purpose and medical VLMs \citep{zhu2026lost,pedersen2026probes}. Activation steering promises the converse operation: write the probe direction back into the representation and move the model's behaviour \citep{turner2023steering,panickssery2024caa}. Together, these operations would make a probe direction a handle on the concept: read it to detect, write it to control. This paper asks whether that handle exists.

Showing that steering ``works'' does not answer the question. A write can change an answer for reasons unrelated to the concept's label. Any perturbation of matched size can push a yes/no answer. A direction fitted for one finding can carry a generic abnormality signal shared with other findings \citep{pahde2025navigating}. Random and sham directions test the first possibility. Testing the second requires a comparison steering studies rarely make: write every competing clinical direction at the same locus and dose in the same patients, then ask whether the concept's own direction moves the concept's own answer more than any other direction. We call the outcome \emph{ownership}. A concept with a readable but unowned probe direction is one the model can be read for but not written with---reading is not writing.

We build an evaluation that tests this distinction at scale (Section~\ref{sec:framework}). At the final visual block whose output the language model consumes, we assign each concept three grades: \emph{readable} if its probe beats matched random-label controls, \emph{answerable} if the model's clean yes/no answer beats chance, and \emph{owned} if a fixed-dose write along its direction beats the 95th percentile of 119 random directions, a coordinate-permutation sham, and all five competing clinical directions under simultaneous max-$T$ bounds. Seven acceptance gates precede block scoring. We evaluate every contrast in one fixed batch composition. every NIH and COCO block reports dose response, refit stability, a second locus, label-shift controls, and prompt-template contrasts. We apply the evaluation to \cfCheckpoints{} checkpoints from nine families (3B--72B; general-purpose and medical) on two chest-radiograph datasets with report-derived labels, NIH ChestX-ray14 and CheXpert Plus, and on COCO objects as a natural-image control (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

The results show a consistent pattern (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Table~\ref{tab:cf-main}, Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). Clinical concepts are read and answered in most cells but owned in \cfChestOwnedPct\%. In two thirds of the cells, a competing clinical direction moves the concept's answer more than the concept's own direction. The seed result reproduces on patients disjoint from every earlier cohort. The same procedure owns \cfCocoOwnedPct\% of COCO cells, so the deficit is specific to clinical concepts, not to the write. A direction fitted to the model's own answers is a handle where the probe direction is not. Its cosine with the probe direction is $0.1$ on chest radiographs against $0.6$ on natural images. Gemma~3 and MedGemma checkpoints of different sizes share one vision tower, so their probes and write vectors are identical across sizes. Yet which concepts they own changes with the language model that reads the write. Ownership is a property of the reader.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (i) A concept-writability evaluation for VLMs that grades readability, answerability, and ownership at the consumed visual block with matched clinical competitors, a random--sham reference family, simultaneous bounds, and registered acceptance gates. (ii) Its first cross-model measurement: \cfCheckpoints{} checkpoints, nine families, three datasets, \cfCells{} scored concept cells, with every control reported per block. (iii) Three findings that hold across the grid: clinical concepts are read and answered far more often than they are owned; the write mechanism is intact on natural objects; and the reader of the representation, not the representation, decides ownership. The evaluation, its scorecards, and the write matrices are released as a benchmark; a new model is added with one adapter naming its consumed block.
