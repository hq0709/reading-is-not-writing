\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A linear probe reads a concept from a representation. Probe-vector steering assigns it a second role: writing that concept back into the model. Qwen's Effusion normal decodes robustly and reproducibly beats random and sham controls, yet a Nodule normal moves the same Effusion answer more under the original yes/no prompt. Independent Mass confirmation adds a second contradiction: its clinical advantage under \emph{show} A/B prompts reverses under \emph{is}, while random directions remain stronger. Clinical attribution therefore depends on the direction--question--prompt combination.

Linear probes expose information available to a restricted readout family \citep{alain2017understanding,hewitt2019designing}; they do not establish the model's use of the decoded property \citep{ravichander2021probing}. Steering can reflect a broadly effective axis, clinical-direction overlap, or locus anisotropy. Random and sham vectors challenge generic effects. Semantic competition asks whether the named direction is privileged among equally constructed clinical directions for the named response.

For a fixed yes/no prompt, let $W_{q,d}$ denote the paired mean change in question $q$'s yes probability under direction $d$. The ownership margin $O_q=W_{q,q}-\max_{d\neq q}W_{q,d}$ compares the named direction with its strongest clinical competitor. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} illustrates this fixed-prompt comparison. Contenders share readout construction, locus, dose, patient rows, and endpoint. Across prompts, finding-present logit margins align answer mappings.

The original six gates establish the fixed-prompt reader--writer boundary on NIH ChestX-ray14. All three LLaVA/Qwen cells are readable, with AUROC 0.7742--0.8009 and positive controlled selectivity. LLaVA directions stop at random/sham controls. On 400 independent patients, Qwen Effusion changes mean $P(\mathrm{yes})$ by 0.1945, versus 0.2519 for Nodule; the familywise ownership contrast is $-0.0573$ (95\% CI $[-0.0694,-0.0450]$). A prospective six-direction by six-question matrix yields zero owned concepts and no shared alias beyond 119 random directions. A fresh 50-patient Consolidation test finds no reliable input-displacement closure: concept gain is 0.0022 versus a random maximum of 0.0025.

The answer-encoding crossover (Section~\ref{sec:encoding-results}) finds mapping-even minus mapping-odd energy of $-2.1786$ (95\% CI $[-2.4061,-1.9810]$) across four calibration-eligible questions. Random and sham directions share odd dominance. A descriptive six-question screen on 200 reused patients finds Mass's clinical logit-effect margin changing from $-0.7181$ under standard yes/no to $+0.2825$ with A-present and $+0.2900$ with B-present.

On 200 independent confirmation patients, Mass's clinical margins are $0.2525$ and $0.2338$ under \emph{show} A-present and B-present, with positive simultaneous lower bounds. Larger effects among 119 random directions defeat both registered specificity criteria. Both \emph{is} coded margins are negative. Explicit \emph{show} yes/no has a similar observed margin; paired A/B contrasts span zero, leaving equivalence unresolved.

Our contributions are threefold: semantic competition formalizes the reader--writer identification problem; the six gates distinguish original steering efficacy from clinical attribution and mechanism confirmation; and independent prompt comparisons establish wording- and instruction-sensitive clinical competition. Attribution requires testing direction, question, and prompt together against clinical and random controls.
