\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A linear probe gives a clinical concept both a score and a vector. That pairing is compelling: decode Effusion, steer the Effusion normal, and observe the model's answer move. In our Qwen experiment, this chain survives two strong checks---the target vector beats matched random directions and a coordinate-permutation sham, then reproduces on 400 independent patients. Yet a fixed Nodule direction moves the same Effusion answer more. The central identification problem is therefore not only whether steering changes behaviour, but whether the change is specific to the clinical concept that names the direction.

Linear probes are useful because they expose information available to a restricted readout family \citep{alain2017understanding,hewitt2019designing}. Their accuracy alone does not establish that the underlying model uses the decoded property for its task \citep{ravichander2021probing}. This distinction is especially consequential in medical vision-language models (VLMs), where a clinical label can make a decoded vector appear operational by construction. Recent medical VLM work traces classification signals across depth \citep{zhu2026lost}, while VLM steering work uses probe vectors to edit visual concepts \citep{theodoridis2026probing}. Together, these lines show that clinical properties can be read and that internal edits can move outputs. What remains unidentified is the semantic ownership of that movement: does the target normal outperform equally strong, clinically meaningful alternatives at the same consumed locus?

We ask a narrow causal question: when a clinical concept is robustly linearly decodable at the final visual block, does its probe normal exert direction-specific influence on the answer? Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-ladder} summarizes the protocol. We first verify the exact final visual block on the model's forward path. At that locus, we fit one capacity-matched probe under a patient split and compare its AUROC with 20 type-to-random-label controls. We then add the unit probe normal to each visual token at a dose proportional to that token's activation norm. Random directions, a coordinate-permutation sham, and fixed unrelated clinical probe normals receive the same dose. The design treats availability, generic perturbation selectivity, and clinical direction specificity as successive evidentiary rungs.

Our evidence consists of three registered model--concept cells on NIH ChestX-ray14. All three target labels are linearly available, with AUROC between 0.7742 and 0.8009 and positive controlled selectivity. The two LLaVA cells identify the first boundary: comparable availability does not determine whether a probe normal exceeds generic controls. Qwen Effusion identifies the second. Its response clears random and sham controls twice, including on a prospectively selected cohort of 400 new patients, while the fixed Nodule direction produces a larger response and places the familywise Effusion-minus-maximum-unrelated interval entirely below zero.

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce a forward-path, capacity-controlled evidence ladder that tests controlled decodability, random/sham intervention selectivity, and clinical direction specificity at one consumed representation.
    \item We expose two distinct gaps in causal interpretation: comparable linear availability can yield different generic-control outcomes, and a generic-control pass can still fail to identify the named clinical direction.
    \item We replicate Qwen's Effusion response on independent patients and use a familywise comparison over fixed clinical directions to show that Nodule is reliably stronger.
\end{itemize}

The resulting principle is practical: causal interpretation of a decoded direction requires semantic competition, not only non-semantic controls. The competing directions must be tested at the same locus, dose, rows, and behavioural endpoint. Sections~\ref{sec:related}--\ref{sec:protocol} place and define this test, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the evidence ladder, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}--\ref{sec:conclusion} give its interpretation and scope.
