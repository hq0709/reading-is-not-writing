\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A successful intervention poses an attribution question: does the direction's training label explain its privileged effect on the answer? Linear probes identify information available to restricted readouts \citep{alain2017understanding,hewitt2019designing}, and decodability can persist without task relevance \citep{ravichander2021probing}. Activation steering goes further by changing a representation and measuring the downstream response \citep{turner2023steering,panickssery2024caa}. Even after this intervention succeeds, assigning its effect to the probe's clinical label requires a comparison with semantic alternatives.

Random directions test generic sensitivity at a matched dose, while a coordinate-permutation sham tests dependence on the direction's coordinate arrangement. Neither asks whether an equally constructed direction bearing another clinical label produces a larger response to the same question. Separability-trained concept vectors can absorb distractor signals \citep{pahde2025navigating}. Semantic competition tests the resulting attribution directly by comparing labeled writers after a controlled write effect has been established.

We study NIH ChestX-ray14 with fixed direction bundles at the consumed final visual blocks of LLaVA-1.5-7B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B. For fixed yes/no prompts, $W_{q,d}$ is the paired mean change in question $q$'s yes probability under direction $d$. Ownership $O_q=W_{q,q}-\max_{d\neq q}W_{q,d}$ compares the named direction with its strongest clinical competitor (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}). Contenders share construction, locus, dose, patients, and endpoint. Ownership denotes relative advantage within this fixed clinical family, not a necessary condition for concept participation in computation. Finding-present logit margins align the endpoint across answer mappings.

Two findings organize the results. Qwen Effusion steering replicates at a locked dose on 400 independent patients beyond random p95 and sham, yet loses to the strongest clinical competitor by $0.0573$ (95\% CI for $O_{\mathrm{Effusion}}$: $[-0.0694,-0.0450]$; Sections~\ref{sec:qwen-replication}--\ref{sec:specificity-results}). Thus successful intervention coexists with a clinical attribution deficit.

Mass's clinical advantage reproduces on 200 independent patients under both \emph{show} A/B mappings, with positive simultaneous lower bounds against all five competitors, while both \emph{is} margins are negative (Section~\ref{sec:encoding-results}). Paired wording analyses are descriptive. Larger effects among 119 random directions defeat both registered specificity criteria. All confirmation AUROC-change intervals cross zero and Brier error increases, leaving diagnostic benefit unsupported.

The remaining experiments delimit these attribution findings through boundary analyses of broader competition, mechanism, LLaVA behavior, and natural paired response (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism-results}).
