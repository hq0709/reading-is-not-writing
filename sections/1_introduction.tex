\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A linear probe fitted on a vision-language model's visual representation reads a clinical concept out of it; steering along the probe normal writes that concept back in and measures how the model's answer responds \citep{alain2017understanding,turner2023steering,panickssery2024caa}. Decodability can persist without task relevance \citep{ravichander2021probing}, and a successful write does not by itself establish that the direction's training label explains the response: random and sham directions test whether an intervention exceeds generic perturbations, but they leave its advantage over equally constructed directions bearing other clinical labels untested. Separability-trained concept vectors can absorb distractor signals \citep{pahde2025navigating}. We therefore ask a sharper question than ``does steering work'': when a model can read a clinical concept, can the same direction be written back so that it moves the concept's own answer more than any competing clinical direction?

We answer it with a fixed evaluation at the visual block the language model consumes. For question $q$ and direction $d$, $W_{q,d}$ is the paired mean change in the yes probability of $q$ under a write along $d$ at a relative dose; \emph{ownership} $O_q=W_{q,q}-\max_{d\neq q}W_{q,d}$ compares the named direction with its strongest clinical competitor (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}). Contenders share construction, locus, dose, patients, and endpoint. A concept is \emph{readable} when its probe beats matched random-label controls, \emph{answerable} when the model's clean answer beats chance, and \emph{owned} when its write beats 119 random directions, a coordinate-permutation sham, and every competitor with simultaneous max-$T$ lower bounds. These three graded outcomes, computed by one protocol with registered acceptance gates, turn the attribution question into a benchmark.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Reading is not writing.} Across 22 checkpoints from nine families (Qwen2.5-VL, Qwen3-VL, InternVL3.5, Gemma~3, MedGemma, Lingshu, LLaVA-1.5, LLaVA-Med, Llama~3.2~Vision; 3B--90B) on NIH ChestX-ray14 and CheXpert Plus, clinical concepts are readable in 55\% and answerable in 61\% of 216 checkpoint--concept cells but owned in 13\% (Section~\ref{sec:campaign}). In the seed model, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Effusion steering reproduces beyond random and sham controls on patients disjoint from every earlier cohort, yet the Nodule direction moves the Effusion answer more ($O_{\mathrm{Effusion}}=-0.071$, 95\% CI $[-0.080,-0.062]$).
\item \textbf{The deficit is clinical, and it belongs to the reader.} The identical procedure on COCO objects owns 87\% of cells with effects up to $0.9$ and monotone dose response, so the write mechanism is intact (Section~\ref{sec:coco}). Gemma~3 (4B/12B/27B) and MedGemma (4B/27B) share one vision tower, so their probes and write vectors are byte-identical across sizes, yet which concepts are owned changes with the language model that reads them (Section~\ref{sec:same-write}).
\item \textbf{A benchmark for concept writability.} The protocol fixes cohorts, loci, direction construction, dose, prompt templates, seven acceptance gates, and decision rules, and reports a per-checkpoint leaderboard of readable, answerable, and owned concepts with all controls (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}; Table~\ref{tab:cf-leaderboard}). Adding a checkpoint requires only an adapter that names its consumed visual block.
\end{enumerate}

The original two-model study (Sections~\ref{sec:qwen-replication}--\ref{sec:encoding-results}) supplies the seed findings; the campaign in Sections~\ref{sec:campaign}--\ref{sec:controls} establishes them as a property of current medical vision-language models rather than of one checkpoint.
