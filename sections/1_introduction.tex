\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Linear probes are the standard instrument for asking what a vision-language model (VLM) represents: fit a classifier on the activations of a chosen layer and read a concept out \citep{alain2017understanding,belinkov2022probing}. In medical imaging the probe often succeeds---clinical findings such as pleural effusion or cardiomegaly are linearly decodable from the visual representation of general-purpose and medical VLMs \citep{zhu2026lost,pedersen2026probes}. Activation steering promises the converse operation: write the probe direction back into the representation and move the model's behaviour \citep{turner2023steering,panickssery2024caa}. Together the two operations would make a probe direction a handle on the concept: read it to detect, write it to control. Whether that handle exists is the question of this paper.

The question is not answered by showing that steering ``works''. A write can change an answer for reasons unrelated to the concept's label: any perturbation of matched size may push a yes/no answer, and a direction fitted for one finding may carry a generic abnormality signal shared with other findings \citep{pahde2025navigating}. Random and sham directions test the first possibility. The second requires a comparison that steering studies rarely make: write every competing clinical direction at the same locus, dose, and patients, and ask whether the concept's own direction moves the concept's own answer more than any other. We call the outcome of that comparison \emph{ownership}. A concept whose probe direction is readable but not owned is one the model can be read for but not written with---reading is not writing.

We build an evaluation that grades this distinction at scale (Section~\ref{sec:framework}). At the final visual block whose output the language model consumes, each concept receives three grades: \emph{readable} if its probe beats matched random-label controls, \emph{answerable} if the model's clean yes/no answer beats chance, and \emph{owned} if a fixed-dose write along its direction beats 119 random directions, a coordinate-permutation sham, and all five competing clinical directions under simultaneous max-$T$ bounds. Seven acceptance gates run before any block is scored, every contrast is evaluated in one fixed batch composition, and every block reports dose response, refit stability, a second locus, label-shift controls, and prompt-template contrasts. We apply the evaluation to 21 checkpoints from nine families (3B--72B; general-purpose and medical) on two chest-radiograph datasets with report-derived labels, NIH ChestX-ray14 and CheXpert Plus, and on COCO objects as a natural-image control (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

The result is a consistent picture (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Table~\ref{tab:cf-main}, Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). Clinical concepts are readable in 55\% of 234 checkpoint--concept cells and answerable in 60\% of the 222 cells with a scored write matrix, but owned in 12\%; in two thirds of the cells a competing clinical direction moves the concept's answer more than the concept's own direction. The seed result that motivated the evaluation reproduces on patients disjoint from every earlier cohort: in Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Effusion steering beats random and sham controls yet the Nodule direction raises the Effusion answer more ($O_{\mathrm{Effusion}}=-0.071$, 95\% CI $[-0.080,-0.062]$). The same procedure on COCO objects owns 87\% of cells with monotone dose response, so the deficit is specific to clinical concepts, not to the write. And because Gemma~3 and MedGemma checkpoints of different sizes share one vision tower, their probes and write vectors are byte-identical across sizes---yet which concepts they own changes with the language model that reads the write. Ownership is a property of the reader.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (i) A concept-writability evaluation for VLMs that grades readability, answerability, and ownership at the consumed visual block with matched clinical competitors, a random--sham reference family, simultaneous bounds, and registered acceptance gates. (ii) The first cross-model measurement of that evaluation: 21 checkpoints, nine families, three datasets, 330 scored concept cells, with every control reported per block. (iii) Three findings that hold across the grid: clinical concepts are read and answered far more often than they are owned; the write mechanism itself is intact on natural objects; and ownership is decided by the reader of the representation rather than by the representation. The evaluation, its per-checkpoint scorecards, and the write matrices are released as a benchmark to which a new model is added with one adapter naming its consumed block.
