\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A linear probe is optimized to read a concept from a representation. Probe-vector steering assigns it a second role: writing that concept back into the model. The two roles need not agree. Qwen's Effusion normal decodes robustly and reproducibly beats random and sham controls, yet a Nodule normal moves the same Effusion answer more under the original yes/no prompt. A further contradiction appears across prompts: Mass's direction trails its strongest clinical competitor under standard yes/no but leads the clinical family under two coded-answer prompts in a descriptive screen. Together, these observations locate clinical attribution in the direction--question--prompt combination.

Linear probes expose information available to a restricted readout family \citep{alain2017understanding,hewitt2019designing}; they do not by themselves show that the model uses the decoded property \citep{ravichander2021probing}. Steering along a labeled normal can reflect a broadly effective axis, overlap among clinical directions, or anisotropy at the intervention locus. Random and sham vectors challenge generic effects. Semantic competition asks a further question: among equally constructed clinical directions, is the named direction privileged for the named response under the tested prompt?

For a fixed yes/no prompt, write $W_{q,d}$ for the paired mean change in question $q$'s yes probability when steering along clinical direction $d$. The ownership margin $O_q=W_{q,q}-\max_{d\neq q}W_{q,d}$ compares the named direction with its strongest clinical competitor. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} illustrates this fixed-prompt comparison. Every contender uses the same readout construction, locus, dose, patient rows, and endpoint. Across answer encodings, clinically oriented logit margins align the finding-present response, allowing the same competition to be examined under each prompt package.

The original six gates establish the fixed-prompt reader--writer boundary on NIH ChestX-ray14. All three LLaVA/Qwen model--concept cells are readable, with AUROC 0.7742--0.8009 and positive controlled selectivity. The LLaVA directions stop at random/sham controls. On 400 independent patients, Qwen Effusion changes mean $P(\mathrm{yes})$ by 0.1945, versus 0.2519 for Nodule; the familywise ownership contrast is $-0.0573$ (95\% CI $[-0.0694,-0.0450]$). A prospective six-direction by six-question matrix yields zero owned concepts and no shared alias beyond 119 random directions. A fresh 50-patient Consolidation test finds no reliable input-displacement closure: concept gain is 0.0022 versus a random maximum of 0.0025.

The answer-encoding crossover extends this analysis to the task interface (Section~\ref{sec:encoding-results}). Across four calibration-eligible questions, mapping-even minus mapping-odd energy is $-2.1786$ (95\% CI $[-2.4061,-1.9810]$). Odd dominance also occurs for random and sham directions, establishing neither clinical specificity nor encoding invariance. Mass's clinical logit-effect margin changes from $-0.7181$ under standard yes/no to $+0.2825$ with A-present and $+0.2900$ with B-present. This descriptive discovery screens six questions on 200 reused intervention patients; wording and response instructions co-vary, and independent confirmation is pending.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate the reader--writer identification problem through \emph{semantic competition} among matched clinical directions. Second, the six-gate sequence separates decodability, non-generic writing, semantic ownership, and mechanism confirmation under the original prompts. Third, the crossover exposes task-interface sensitivity as part of clinical attribution: the clinical ranking must be evaluated for a direction, question, and prompt together. This formulation makes the prompt an explicit condition of interpretation, with prospective confirmation testing whether the observed advantage generalizes to independent patients.
