\section{Prompts, Scores, and Notation}
\label{app:protocol}

The appendix develops the evidence behind the reader--writer comparison.
This section specifies the original endpoint and notation;
Appendix~\ref{app:runs} identifies the cohorts and computation behind each
experiment. Appendices~\ref{app:ownership} and~\ref{app:specificity-closure}
examine semantic competition and paired input closure in detail.
Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} checks that the interventions enter the
consumed forward path and that their results replay.
Appendix~\ref{app:decoding} compares the original intervention outcomes and
reader selectivity. Appendices~\ref{app:response-diagnostics} and~\ref{app:encoding}
then examine score-coordinate diagnostics and answer-encoding sensitivity;
Appendix~\ref{app:mass-confirmation} tests the Mass candidate on independent
patients across question wordings and response instructions.
Appendix~\ref{app:paired-opportunity} compares paired Consolidation response
on a shared cohort across two model configurations.

\subsection{A common question and answer endpoint}

At the first answer position, let $\ell_{\mathrm{yes}}$ and
$\ell_{\mathrm{no}}$ denote the maximum log probabilities among the valid
single-token case and leading-space variants of each answer.  The registered
score is $P(\mathrm{yes})=\sigma(\ell_{\mathrm{yes}}-\ell_{\mathrm{no}})$,
where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-score} shows
how the six ownership questions share their wording and scoring pipeline:
only the finding phrase changes. The original Edema cell uses ``pulmonary
edema,'' and the closure experiment uses ``consolidation'' in the same
template. Taking the best valid token variant for each answer makes the
endpoint insensitive to which listed capitalization or leading-space form
receives the highest score. This endpoint measures the relative support for
yes versus no at the first answer position; it does not measure the accuracy
of a generated clinical report.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/figA1_prompt_score.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The registered prompts share one template and one answer score.}
The six findings instantiate an otherwise fixed yes/no question, and every
model uses the same first-answer-position scoring sequence.}
\label{fig:prompt-score}
\end{figure}

The shared interface makes the question--direction grid interpretable:
changing a clinical direction does not change the question being scored.
Differences across columns still reflect different clinical questions, so
the ownership test compares directions within each column before drawing
a conclusion about the named concept.

\subsection{Reading the intervention notation}

Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects the notation introduced in the article.
The distinction between question $q$ and direction $d$ is central: a large
$W_{q,d}$ shows that a direction moves an answer, while $O_q$ asks whether
the direction bearing that answer's clinical name moves it more than its
competitors. A positive ownership margin alone is not the full confirmation
rule; the uncertainty and non-semantic control comparisons must also pass.
Keeping these roles separate allows the following analyses to distinguish
readability, intervention strength, and semantic attribution.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Notation used in the manuscript.}
\label{tab:notation}
\resizebox{\ifdim\width>\linewidth\linewidth\else\width\fi}{!}{%
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Symbol & Definition \\
\midrule
$q,d,c$ & Clinical question, steering direction, and probe concept, respectively. \\
$\hvec_{it}$ & Consumed visual representation for image $i$ and visual token $t$. \\
$R,\boldsymbol{s}$ & Fixed Gaussian projection and train-fitted featurewise scale. \\
$\hat\wvec_c$ & Unit probe-normal direction for concept $c$ after lifting to the consumed representation. \\

The notation separates three levels of evidence: $S$ describes label
availability, $W_{q,d}$ describes an intervention effect, and $O_q$ compares
the named direction with clinical alternatives. The remaining appendix
follows this progression while keeping the evaluation cohorts explicit.

\section{Complete Causal-Ownership Surface}
\label{app:ownership}

\subsection{Question-specific effects and clinical competitors}

Here $q$ indexes a clinical question, $d$ indexes a steering direction, and
$W_{q,d}$ retains its definition from Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.  Rows in
Figure~\ref{fig:ownership-matrix} are directions and columns are questions, so
the named comparison lies on the diagonal.  The column-wise ownership margin
$O_q$ is positive only when that diagonal exceeds every clinical competitor.
The off-diagonal maxima reveal why a strong write effect does not identify
its clinical owner. For example, the Mass answer changes by 0.511 under the
Effusion direction versus 0.359 under its own direction, and the Effusion
answer changes by 0.245 under Nodule versus 0.181 under Effusion. All six
columns have an off-diagonal winner, so the mismatch extends beyond the
original Effusion comparison.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/figA3_ownership_matrix.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Every clinical question is moved most by an off-diagonal
direction.} Outlined cells mark the named diagonals, and orange triangles
mark each column's strongest clinical direction. The continuous color scale
encodes signed effects, with white at zero; printed values give the
corresponding estimates to three decimal places.}
\label{fig:ownership-matrix}
\end{figure}

The surface shows substantial answer movement alongside a systematic
failure of diagonal dominance. Several questions share the same strongest
direction, which motivates both the per-question ownership test below and
the subsequent test for a shared alias. A visually strong row alone cannot
settle either claim.

Table~\ref{tab:ownership-statistics} quantifies these comparisons with
patient-bootstrap uncertainty. ``Best other'' is the strongest alternative
clinical effect for the question in that row; ``Sim. LCB'' reports the
simultaneous lower confidence bound for $O_q$, using the max-$T$ procedure
over the clinical contrasts. The descriptive intervals also apply to $O_q$.
The reported margins range from $-0.230$ to
$-0.064$, and every descriptive 95\% interval lies below zero. The named
diagonals also fall below their corresponding random maxima. Thus neither
clinical competition nor the random-control comparison supports a named
owner. The displayed effects and margins are rounded independently from
the full-precision estimates.

\subsection{Testing a shared answer-sensitive direction}

Figure~\ref{fig:shared-alias} asks whether a direction that lacks a specific
clinical owner nevertheless acts as a shared answer-sensitive alias. Panel
(a) compares signed mean off-diagonal effects across directions; panel (b)
places the strongest candidate against the global controls.
Effusion has mean effect 0.2909 and positive simultaneous lower bounds for
effect and relative dominance, 0.2808 and 0.1332. Its candidate effect still
falls below the global random alias maximum of 0.3402. This control first
averages each random direction's effect over each five-question
off-diagonal set, then takes the maximum over all 119 directions and six
sets. It is therefore distinct from the individual-question maxima in
Table~\ref{tab:ownership-statistics}. The sham control is the maximum
absolute mean effect across individual questions. Because alias
confirmation requires all of these comparisons to pass, the broad response
pattern does not confirm the shared-alias hypothesis.

\subsection{From paired input displacement to closure}

For the paired closure gate, $\gamma_i$ is the positive-minus-negative
representation displacement for patient $i$, and $\alpha_i$ is the
displacement-matched dose applied to that patient's negative study.  The
closure gain measures the reduction in the absolute paired
$P(\mathrm{yes})$ gap.  These quantities are separated into distinct panels
in Figure~\ref{fig:input-closure} because they have different scales.
Panel (a), reader eligibility, establishes that Consolidation is readable
before pair selection. Panel (b), paired displacement, shows a positive mean
input displacement, but its lower confidence bound is below zero. The
closure-gains panel (c) shows that the concept's gain of 0.00220 is below the
random maximum of 0.00246; the clinical-margin panel (d) adds a negative lower
bound for the clinical contrast. The random-rank $p$-value of 0.025 is descriptive
and does not replace the registered requirement to exceed every random
direction. The joint closure criterion therefore remains unmet despite the
eligible reader and positive point estimates.

\section{Forward-Path and Replay Checks}
\label{app:implementation}

\subsection{Does the write reach the consumed pathway?}

Figure~\ref{fig:forward-path} locates the intervention relative to the
connector or merger and answer logits. A captured activation must be
consumed downstream for its modification to test the model's behavior.
The zero-dose check tests whether installing the hook changes the
unperturbed computation; the nonzero-dose checks test whether a write
propagates beyond the captured block. The diagram locates both checks
along the forward path and shows where their downstream witnesses are
measured.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/figA6_forward_path.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{A valid intervention must enter the consumed forward path
and reach the answer logits.} The same hook is tested at zero and nonzero dose
before its scientific outcomes are interpreted.}
\label{fig:forward-path}
\end{figure}

The path checks distinguish a valid manipulation from a change to an
unused activation. They make the observed answer effects attributable to
the implemented write operation, while leaving the direction's semantic
meaning to the experimental controls. The model-specific witnesses provide
the corresponding implementation evidence.

changes; Qwen's final visual block likewise affects both the merger and
answer logits at $\alpha=+0.1$. The zero-dose column tests baseline
preservation, and the final two columns test propagation through the
model-specific connector and answer computation.
