\section{Implications}
\label{sec:implications}

\paragraph{For steering, model building, and clinical evaluation.}
A probe direction that reads a clinical concept is not a handle on it. Probe accuracy and answer accuracy, the two quantities most often reported for medical VLMs, do not establish whether a handle exists. Both are high in this grid, yet ownership is 12\%. A steering study that reports a large answer change against random and sham controls shows that the write reaches the answer. It does not show that the label names the direction. At the same locus, dose, and patients, a direction fitted for another finding moves the same answer as much or more in two thirds of clinical cells. The matched-competitor comparison is the missing test, cheap once the competitors' directions exist. Studies using probe normals as interventions must report the write matrix $W$. They must compare ownership $O_q$ across layers, doses, and models. Every clinical write must include a natural-image control so a failed handle is not mistaken for a failed mechanism. A checkpoint whose Effusion direction moves the Effusion answer less than its Nodule direction does has no Effusion handle at this locus. The direction it responds to is fitted to its own answers, at cosine $0.1$ to the probe direction. The same write vector produces different ownership in different language models. A vision tower that exposes a finding linearly therefore does not make its direction a handle. On the chest sets, ownership rises with the reader in some families (Qwen2.5-VL from 7B to 72B, Lingshu from 7B to 32B) and not in others (Gemma~3, InternVL3.5). Ownership appears most often with medical fine-tuning of the reader. The benchmark grades a new checkpoint in about ten GPU-hours per chest dataset. An evaluation that certifies concept use for deployment must include this test.
