\section{Implications}
\label{sec:implications}

\paragraph{For steering, model building, and clinical evaluation.}
A probe direction that reads a clinical concept is not a handle on it. Probe accuracy and answer accuracy, the two quantities most often reported for medical VLMs, do not establish whether a handle exists. Both are high in this grid, yet ownership is \cfChestOwnedPct\%. Beating random and sham controls shows that a steering write reaches the answer, not that the direction belongs to the labelled finding. Of the \cfChestRefMet{} clinical cells whose own write clears those controls, \cfChestRefStrong{} lose to a direction fitted for another finding at the same locus and dose in the same patients. A further \cfChestRefUnres{} are unresolved, leaving \cfChestOwned{} owned. Once competitors' directions exist, the matched-competitor test is cheap. Studies using probe normals as interventions should report the write matrix $W$, compare ownership $O_q$ across layers, doses, and models, and pair every clinical write with a natural-image control. The same write vector produces different ownership across language models, and swapping the vision tower behind a fixed reader does not restore a clinical handle either. A vision tower that exposes a finding linearly therefore does not make its direction a handle, and neither does a reader trained on medical text. Ownership has to be measured on the pair that will be deployed.
